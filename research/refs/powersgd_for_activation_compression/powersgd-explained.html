Distributed optimization · low-rank gradient compression

PowerSGD

Send a cheap low-rank sketch of the gradient instead of the whole thing — and remember the part you dropped, so nothing is lost.

gradient singular values are “top-heavy” — a few directions carry almost everything

After Vogels, Karimireddy & Jaggi — Practical Low-Rank Gradient Compression for Distributed Optimization (NeurIPS 2019)

01 — THE BOTTLENECK

Why distributed training is communication-limited

In data-parallel SGD, W worker GPUs each compute a gradient on their own slice of data, then must average those gradients every single step before updating the model.

That average means shipping a vector the size of the entire model across the network, every step, forever. As models grow and worker counts climb, the network — not the GPUs — sets the pace. The compute sits idle, waiting on the wire. PowerSGD attacks exactly this: shrink what gets sent, without hurting the final accuracy, and without the compression itself costing more than the bytes it saves.

02 — THE IDEA

A gradient is a matrix. Most of it is redundant.

Take one layer's gradient and treat it as a matrix M of shape n×m. The bet — well supported for deep nets — is that this matrix is approximately low rank: a handful of directions hold almost all of its energy. So approximate it as a product of two skinny matrices:

Instead of n·m numbers you send only r·(n+m). Because a matrix's area grows far faster than its perimeter, the saving is enormous on real layers:

What counts as a matrix

Fully-connected layers are matrices already. A convolution's gradient is a 4-D tensor like 512×512×3×3; flatten the last three dims and it becomes 512×4608. Bias vectors are tiny, so they're sent uncompressed.

03 — THE COMPRESSION STEP

The heart of it — and the “two Q” that confuses everyone

PowerSGD finds the sketch with a single step of subspace iteration. It involves a matrix called Q that appears to show up twice. It doesn't — it's the same object playing two roles, at two moments:

The one thing to understand

Qin is a guess — a probe. You don't trust it. You only use it to ask the gradient “which directions do you mostly point in?” by computing P = M·Qin, then cleaning the result up (orthonormalizing) into P̂. That P̂ is the answer: the gradient's main directions.

Qout is the answer you keep. Now that you know the good directions P̂, you compute the matching coefficients Qout = Mᵀ·P̂. This is what actually reconstructs the gradient, and this is what you transmit. And next step, Qout becomes the new Qin — the answer cycles around to become the guess. That's why it's the same letter.

Why orthonormalize? Once P̂ has perpendicular, unit-length columns, Qout = Mᵀ·P̂ is exactly the least-squares best set of coefficients, because P̂·Qoutᵀ is precisely the projection of M onto the directions P̂ spans. A useful side effect: only the direction of Qin matters, never its size — orthonormalizing throws the size away. That's exactly why reusing last step's answer as this step's probe is harmless.

04 — WARM START

Why one step is enough

A single step of subspace iteration from a random guess gives a rough sketch — normally you'd need many iterations to converge to the true best rank-r approximation (an SVD). PowerSGD does exactly one, which is what keeps it cheap. The trick that rescues quality is to seed each step with last step's Qout instead of re-randomizing.

This works because the model takes tiny steps, so consecutive gradients point in nearly the same directions — the dominant subspace drifts slowly. One iteration from a warm start lands about where many iterations from a cold start would. (As a bonus, reusing the old basis averages over consecutive stochastic gradients, like Oja's rule, quietly reducing variance.)

Important

Q is updated every step but it is not trainable. No gradient flows into it; it never appears in the loss. It's bookkeeping the compressor recomputes by its own rule — the same category as a momentum buffer or Adam's running averages. It adapts (it tracks the gradient subspace), but through power iteration, not backprop.

05 — ERROR FEEDBACK

The non-negotiable part

A low-rank projection is biased: the discarded tail is structured, not random noise. Drop it and it's gone forever, and the model converges as if those directions had no gradient. Error feedback fixes this by keeping a buffer e the full size of the gradient. You never compress the raw gradient — you compress g + e, then set e to whatever didn't make it through:

Δ    = g + e                       # fold in last step's leftover
sent = decompress(compress(Δ))     # what actually goes on the wire
e    = Δ − sent                    # remember the residual for next time

Nothing is ever permanently dropped. Whatever the rank-r sketch can't capture this step accumulates in e and gets folded into a later step, where it can finally grow large enough to enter the top-r subspace and be sent. Step through it and watch the backlog build, then discharge:

The invariant behind it: the total you actually transmit equals the total true gradient, lagging by exactly whatever is sitting in e right now (Σ sent = t·G − e). That is what “a biased compressor, made unbiased over time” means concretely. Skip error feedback and rank-4 PowerSGD plateaus around 88% where it should reach 94.4% — the gap is the lost tail. The cost is memory: e is full gradient size, one per worker.

06 — LINEARITY & ALL-REDUCE

The property that makes it scale

Both multiplications — P = M·Q and Q = Mᵀ·P̂ — are linear. So compressing the average of the workers' gradients equals the average of their compressions: compression commutes with averaging.

That single fact lets PowerSGD ride the all-reduce collective — the operation that sums while it communicates, in a tree, scaling as O(log W), leaving every worker with the already-averaged result and a decode cost that doesn't grow with worker count. Sign-based methods and top-K are not linear (the sign of a sum isn't the sum of signs), so they're stuck with all-gather: every worker collects all W messages and decodes them one by one, at O(W). This is the real reason PowerSGD scales where other compressors stall.

07 — THE WHOLE THING

One step of real PowerSGD

Putting the three ingredients together — the linear sketch, warm start, and error feedback — here is what each worker does every step. Two pieces of state ride across steps; neither is trainable.

  • 1g = local stochastic gradient  # shape n×m
  • 2Δ = g + e  # (1) error feedback in
  • 3(P̂, Q) = COMPRESS(Δ, Q)  # one subspace step, warm-started; Q updated here
  • 4e = Δ − DECOMPRESS(P̂, Q)  # (2) error buffer = residual not captured
  • 5Δ′ = DECOMPRESS( ALL-REDUCE )  # sketches averaged in-flight — the linearity trick
  • 6m = γ·m + Δ′  # momentum
  • 7x = x − η·(Δ′ + m)  # the only thing actually trained

The two carried buffers are Q (shape m×r, the warm-start basis) and e (shape n×m, the error memory). Both are recomputed every step by the rules above; neither receives a gradient. Only the model weights x are trained.

08 — IN ONE BREATH

Three ingredients, three payoffs

Linear low-rank sketch → rides all-reduce, so it scales with workers and fast networks.

Warm start → one cheap power-iteration step matches the expensive best rank-r approximation, so quality is essentially free.

Error feedback → the discarded tail is remembered and paid back, so the biased compressor is unbiased over time and reaches full SGD accuracy.

Together: the same number of iterations, the same final accuracy, a fraction of the bytes — and the advantage grows for larger models, which is exactly where communication dominates.
