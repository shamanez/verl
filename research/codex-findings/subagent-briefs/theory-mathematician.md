## 1. Notation

Let `theta_t` be the current fast-policy parameters and `pi_t = pi_{theta_t}`. A stale anchor delay of `K` means the anchor computes with

```text
s = t - K,
theta_s = theta_{t-K},
pi_s = pi_{theta_s},
D_s = D(pi_s).
```

Here `D(pi)` is the rollout distribution induced by policy `pi`, including prompts, generated completions, token histories, rewards, group-relative normalization, masks, and any PPO/GRPO bookkeeping. The current on-policy GRPO target is an expected gradient of the schematic form

```text
g_t = G(theta_t, D_t),        D_t = D(pi_t),
```

where `G(theta, D)` means: evaluate the GRPO/PPO-style score, clipping, and KL terms at parameter point `theta` on samples and advantages drawn from distribution `D`.

The paired stale anchor gradient is

```text
M_s = g_hat(theta_s; B_s),    B_s ~ D_s,
E[M_s | theta_s] = G(theta_s, D_s)
```

assuming the stored rollout batch `B_s` really was produced by the same stale policy snapshot `theta_s`. That is internally on-policy for the old policy. It is not on-policy for `theta_t`.

## 2. Clean stale-error decomposition

Ignoring finite-batch noise first, the expected stale error is

```text
E_stale = G(theta_s, D_s) - G(theta_t, D_t).
```

Insert the mixed term `G(theta_t, D_s)`:

```text
E_stale
  = [G(theta_s, D_s) - G(theta_t, D_s)]
  + [G(theta_t, D_s) - G(theta_t, D_t)].
```

The two terms are distinct:

1. Parameter-point drift:

```text
Delta_param = G(theta_s, D_s) - G(theta_t, D_s).
```

This is the ordinary stale-gradient error from evaluating the gradient field at `theta_s` instead of `theta_t`, holding the old rollout distribution fixed. Under local smoothness in `theta`,

```text
Delta_param ~= - H_Ds(theta_s) (theta_t - theta_s) + higher-order terms,
||Delta_param|| <= L_theta ||theta_t - theta_s||.
```

2. Rollout-distribution drift:

```text
Delta_dist = G(theta_t, D_s) - G(theta_t, D_t).
```

This is the on-policy RL-specific term. It remains even if the gradient is re-evaluated at `theta_t` on the stale responses. If the per-sample gradient contribution is bounded by `B`, then a crude total-variation bound gives

```text
||Delta_dist|| <= 2 B TV(D_s, D_t)
              <= B sqrt(2 KL(D_s || D_t)).
```

For trajectories, `KL(D_s || D_t)` can grow roughly with response length and accumulated token-level logprob drift, so a small per-token shift can still produce a large rollout-distribution shift.

With finite batches:

```text
M_s - G(theta_t, D_t)
  = sample_noise_s + Delta_param + Delta_dist,
sample_noise_s = g_hat(theta_s; B_s) - G(theta_s, D_s).
```

Paired replay mainly controls `sample_noise_s` and internal consistency. It does not remove either deterministic stale-bias term.

## 3. Why transplanted on-policy gradients become off-policy

For the stale policy, `M_s` is an on-policy policy-gradient estimate because both the score point and the sampled rollouts come from `pi_s`:

```text
responses ~ D(pi_s),
score term uses grad_theta log pi_theta(response | prompt) at theta = theta_s,
advantages/reward normalization are those induced by the stale group.
```

When that vector is applied as an optimizer signal at `theta_t`, the intended target is instead

```text
responses ~ D(pi_t),
score term at theta = theta_t,
advantages induced by current-policy groups.
```

Thus the same numeric vector changes status: it is on-policy for an old objective and off-policy for the current objective. The off-policy issue is not merely that old samples are reused; it is that GRPO's estimator couples samples, logprobs, group-relative advantages, clipping ratios, and KL penalties to the behavior policy. A stale vector `M_s` is therefore a gradient of a defunct local surrogate, not a current local descent/ascent direction unless the policy and rollout distributions have barely moved.

## 4. What paired replay fixes and does not fix

Paired replay is the statistically correct way to ask the anchor for the old clean gradient:

```text
use theta_s with the same stored batch B_s generated by pi_s.
```

It fixes:

- The anchor does not introduce a second Monte Carlo rollout sample.
- The old weights, old responses, old logprobs, masks, and old group statistics can be kept mutually consistent.
- The result estimates `G(theta_s, D_s)` rather than a hybrid of old weights and new trajectories.

It does not fix:

- The parameter-point gap `theta_s` versus `theta_t`.
- The rollout-distribution gap `D_s` versus `D_t`.
- Missing current-policy advantages, logprob ratios, support, and response-length behavior.
- Any implementation mismatch where `B_s` and `theta_s` are not actually the same generator state.

If one instead re-evaluates the stale batch at `theta_t`, the parameter-point component is reduced, but the estimator remains off-policy because the batch still came from `D_s`, not `D_t`.

## 5. Trust-region and importance-sampling implications

The safe use of stale rollouts requires a small behavior-target gap. A trust-region condition should be stated in distributional terms:

```text
KL(D_s || D_t) small,
or token/logprob ratios pi_t(y | x) / pi_s(y | x) concentrated near 1.
```

PPO-style clipping is a bias-variance compromise for nearby behavior policies. At large delay, most ratios may saturate the clip range, so the update becomes either biased, low-effective-sample, or both. The relevant gate is not just parameter distance `||theta_t - theta_s||`; it is also response-distribution drift, advantage drift, and trajectory-level ratio concentration.

Full importance sampling would require trajectory ratios

```text
rho(t,s;tau) = P_{pi_t}(tau) / P_{pi_s}(tau)
```

and current-policy value/advantage targets. In long language-model rollouts, trajectory ratios can have extreme variance and support problems. Per-token or clipped ratios are more stable but biased. Therefore IS/trust-region correction is credible only as a gated small-drift method, not as a general license to reuse large-`K` anchor gradients.

Practical implication: if stale gradients are used at all, reject or strongly down-weight them when measured KL/logprob drift, ratio variance, or effective sample size crosses a threshold. A stale gradient with poor current cosine is not rescued by being internally clean.

## 6. The `sigma(M)` ceiling

Let `M` be the stale anchor vector and `g = G(theta_t, D_t)` the current target. Any deterministic reuse rule that only transforms `M` and deterministic metadata available at the same stale information level produces

```text
u = f(M),     u is sigma(M)-measurable.
```

Under squared-error loss, the best possible predictor of `g` using only `M` is the conditional expectation

```text
u_star = E[g | sigma(M)].
```

No deterministic scalar reweighting, accumulation, sign filter, or error-feedback transform of `M` can create information about components of `g` that are conditionally independent of `M`. In a one-vector scalar correction, the oracle coefficient is

```text
lambda_star = <g, M> / ||M||^2,
```

so if the stale-current cosine is near zero or negative, the best scalar use is tiny, zero, or sign-reversing. Without an oracle estimate of that cosine, deterministic reuse can easily add coherent bias.

This is the `sigma(M)` ceiling: stale-gradient algebra can denoise, rescale, average, or delay the information already present in `M`, but it cannot manufacture missing current-policy information. To exceed that ceiling, the method needs extra current information: current rollout samples, current logprob/ratio measurements, live compressed gradients, curvature or second-moment information, cross-rank statistics, or a learned predictor that proves out-of-sample stale-to-live cosine lift.

Error feedback is especially dangerous when the residual is biased. In compression with a fixed objective, residual accumulation can preserve lost gradient mass. In stale on-policy GRPO, the residual may preserve old-policy directionality, so accumulation can turn a transient stale bias into a persistent optimizer force.

## 7. Why SFT/fixed-data stale-gradient intuition differs

For supervised fine-tuning on a fixed dataset `D0`,

```text
F(theta) = E_{z ~ D0} loss(theta, z),
g_s = grad F(theta_s),
g_t = grad F(theta_t).
```

The only stale error is parameter-point drift:

```text
g_s - g_t ~= - H(theta_s) (theta_t - theta_s).
```

Async-SGD and error-feedback analyses can then rely on a stationary objective, Lipschitz gradients, bounded delay, and telescoping residuals against the same loss landscape.

GRPO is different:

```text
J_t(theta) depends on D(pi_t),
M_s estimates G(theta_s, D(pi_s)),
current target is G(theta_t, D(pi_t)).
```

The data distribution, generated completions, group-relative baselines, response lengths, and logprob ratios all move with the policy. There is no single fixed empirical risk whose stale gradients simply arrive late. This is why SFT-style "a stale full gradient is still useful information about the same objective" intuition does not transfer cleanly.

## 8. Theoretical bottom line for the final report

The mathematically precise claim is not "stale gradients are always bad." It is:

```text
paired stale anchor gradients are clean estimates of old on-policy gradients,
but biased estimates of current on-policy GRPO gradients.
```

The bias decomposes into parameter-point drift plus rollout-distribution drift. Paired replay fixes internal old-policy validity, not current-policy validity. Trust-region or IS correction can only be trusted inside a measured small-drift regime; outside it, clipping becomes biased and full IS becomes high-variance. Deterministic reuse of a stale vector is capped by the information in `sigma(M)`, so at large delay it should be treated as a gated, down-weighted auxiliary signal at most, not a primary optimizer signal.

For large or variable delay, the theory favors demoting the anchor toward roles that do not require current on-policy gradient validity: codec/Q calibration, measurement, diagnostics, or carefully gated low-dose correction whose usefulness is proven by current-cosine or ratio-based offline tests.
