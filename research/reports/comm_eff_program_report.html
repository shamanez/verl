EXP-25: The Core Idea — Two Circuits for Training a Language Model Over a Slow Link

This project asks whether you can train a reasoning model with reinforcement learning while compressing the data that flows between machines, and still match an uncompressed run. EXP-25 built the realistic "anchor circuit" that does the compressing — a fast compressed path running every step, plus a slow, deliberately-out-of-date uncompressed reference that steers it — and then tested one specific way of folding the two together. That fold (the signed_ema merger) was a bet, and EXP-25 was designed to confirm or kill it.

The problem: training is bottlenecked on communication, not compute

We are training Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (a 1.5-billion-parameter language model) on GSM8K (grade-school math word problems) with GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization, a reinforcement-learning method where, for each problem, the model generates several candidate answers (a rollout), each is scored right or wrong, and the model is nudged toward the answers that scored above the group's average. No reward model, no KL penalty, no entropy bonus: just "be more like the answers that worked."

To fit a 16,000-token answer with 8 rollouts per problem, the model is split across several GPUs using pipeline parallelism: the network's layers are cut into stages, each living on a different device, and a batch flows through them like an assembly line. The catch is the seams. At every stage boundary, the full activations (the hidden vectors the network passes forward) go out on the forward pass and gradients (the error signals) come back on the backward pass. Each hidden vector here is \(H = 1536\) numbers wide, times every token in every sequence. On a fast datacenter interconnect that traffic is free; over a slow link between distant machines — the decentralized setting this project targets — it is the bottleneck. The whole point is to shrink the traffic at those seams during training.

Why compress activations, not weights? Weight-compression schemes (quantize the model, sync less often) are well-trodden. The seam traffic in pipeline parallelism is different: it is the activations and gradients crossing stage boundaries every single step, and it scales with batch size and sequence length, not model size. That is the traffic this project attacks. And it does so on the training path only — the rollouts (answer generation, done by vLLM) stay fully uncompressed. Generation quality is sacred; we only squeeze the learning signal.

The two-circuit design

EXP-25's substrate splits the actor's update into two coupled circuits that share the same process, the same batch, and the same optimizer.

The fast circuit runs every step. At the pipeline-boundary layers it replaces each full hidden vector with a low-rank approximation: a PowerSGD-style projection. Concretely, a shared, skinny orthonormal matrix \(Q\) (the basis, a small set of \(r=77\) direction vectors that everyone agrees on) compresses each activation \(h\) down to its coordinates in that basis, \(Y = hQ\) — only 77 numbers per token instead of 1536 — and reconstructs it on the other side as \(\hat{h} = YQ^{\top}\). Only \(Y\) crosses the wire. This is lossy, so the gradient it produces, call it \(G_{\text{comp}}\), is biased and noisy.

The anchor circuit runs only every cadence = 5 optimizer ticks. It takes a clone of the model with no compression hooks, loads a deliberately stale snapshot of the weights (frozen delay_K = 5 ticks in the past), and runs one clean, uncompressed forward and backward pass. From that it gets two things: a full, exact, full-coverage gradient (covering all 196 weight matrices of the network, averaged across data-parallel ranks), which it folds into a running average \(M\); and the activations it needs to recompute the shared basis \(Q\). Crucially, the anchor is the only thing allowed to update \(Q\) — the fast circuit is a read-only consumer of the basis and is fail-closed from ever writing it.

The analogy. Picture a courier (the fast circuit) sprinting a compressed, slightly-garbled message every minute, and a careful clerk (the anchor) who, every few minutes, writes out one perfect copy — but using a snapshot of the ledger from several minutes ago. The clerk's copy is always a little out of date, yet it is the only fully-correct reference in the building, so it is what everyone uses to recalibrate the courier's shorthand (the basis \(Q\)) and to sanity-check the garbled messages.

Why the staleness is mandatory, not a bug

The earlier, simpler idea was a periodic fresh clean step (clean_cadence): every few steps, just do one full uncompressed update. It worked — it recovered dense-comparable results — which proved the learning signal is recoverable, not destroyed by compression. But it cheats. A fresh full-rank step means shipping the entire uncompressed \(H\)-wide gradient, which is exactly the traffic we are trying to avoid, and on a real slow link that reference would arrive late anyway. So EXP-25 replaced the fresh clean step with the stale anchor: a low-frequency, deliberately-delayed, full-gradient reference maintained continuously. The lateness (delay_K = 5) is the honest model of reality — a far-away clerk's ledger is always behind — so the staleness is the whole point, not a defect.

The merger: EXP-25's actual bet

The two circuits meet in the merger, which folds the anchor's stale-but-clean gradient average \(M\) into the fast circuit's noisy \(G_{\text{comp}}\) — per coordinate, just before the AdamW optimizer sees it. Which way they merge is the open research question. EXP-25 bet on the signed_ema rule:

$$ G_{\text{corr}} \;=\; \alpha \cdot G_{\text{comp}} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\cdot \lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert \cdot \operatorname{sign}(M). $$

In words: keep the magnitude of each entry of the fast gradient (\(\lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert\), how big the step is on that coordinate), but blend in the sign (the direction, up or down) from the trustworthy anchor average \(M\). The mixing knob \(\alpha\) interpolates: at \(\alpha = 1\) the correction is off and you get plain PowerSGD; at \(\alpha = 0\) the direction of every coordinate is fully dictated by the anchor's sign. The hope was that the fast circuit knows roughly how hard to push on each weight, while the clean (if stale) anchor knows which way to push — and that combining them would beat the noisy compressed gradient alone.

EXP-26: The Core Idea — Measure the Geometry Before You Spend on the Next Guess

EXP-25 spent a full multi-arm training campaign to falsify one bad idea (the "signed_ema" merger). EXP-26 refused to make the same trade: before committing any expensive training run, it ran a cheap geometry audit on real gradients to decide which guess was worth the GPU-hours. That single discipline — diagnose first, then spend — is the whole story of this experiment, and it led to the best realistic communication-efficient result the program has produced (val@50 of 0.7210), still 2 points short of parity, with a precise reason why.

The setup, in one paragraph

The trainer compresses the activations that cross a pipeline-stage boundary by projecting each one onto a shared rank-77 basis \(Q\) (the activation space is 1536-dimensional, so this is a 20× squeeze). Compression loses information, so an anchor circuit runs alongside: every few optimizer ticks it does one full, uncompressed forward/backward from a deliberately stale weight snapshot, uses that to refresh and own \(Q\), and maintains a running average \(M\) of the anchor's true gradient. A merger then folds \(M\) into the cheap compressed gradient \(G_{\text{comp}}\) before the optimizer sees it, producing the corrected gradient \(G_{\text{corr}}\). EXP-25's merger replaced the sign of the compressed gradient with the sign of \(M\); EXP-26 set out to understand exactly why that failed and what to try instead. The substrate wiring is in CODE_WALKTHROUGH.md (the anchor "owns Q" rule, the merger math, and the ordering invariant).

The meta-idea. After an expensive falsification, do not "guess a merger and burn 50 GPU-hours." Instead, dump the real tensors the question turns on — the activations, their reconstructions, the basis \(Q\), the compressed gradient, the merger's output, and the anchor's gradient — in full fp32, from a run that lasts only about 10 optimizer ticks, and let the geometry decide which expensive arm runs next. The plan (research/.claude/plans/26.md) makes Step A a hard gate: Steps B/C/E do not launch until the audit returns a machine-readable decision.

Step A: three questions, one cheap diagnostic

The audit (research/runs/EXP-26/stepA_decision.md) tested three hypotheses about where EXP-25's deficit actually lived. Each one is a claim about angles and energy in gradient space, not about a training curve — that is the point of measuring geometry.

An honest caveat baked into the audit. The textbook discriminator — the angle between the compressed update and a true dense update — turned out not to be cleanly measurable here. The anchor's reference gradient comes from a clean policy-gradient loss, while the fast path's gradient comes from the clipped PPO loss, and PowerSGD compresses the boundary activation gradient rather than the weight gradient. Those two mismatches push the literal cosine toward zero for reasons that have nothing to do with compression. The team documented this rather than papering over it, and switched to a confound-free discriminator, \(\cos(G_{\text{comp}}, G_{\text{corr}})\), the merger versus its own input, which never references a dense gradient (research/runs/EXP-26/stepA_verdict.md).

The staged sequence that followed

Everything after Step A was conditional on Step A. The flow below is the actual order of operations — note that two of the four stages cost no extra training runs at all.

  1. Step A — geometry audit (about 10 ticks). Dump real fp32 tensors from three reference configs (dense, plain compression, the old signed_ema merger) on identical prompts. Decide H1/H2/H3. Outcome: go_C_then_B + retire sign-replacement.
  2. Step C — basis-content sweep (one short run). Because H2 was open, six candidate "families" for what \(Q\) should span were built passively inside a single run and scored against the same captured reference gradients (research/runs/EXP-26/STEP_C_SPEC.md). The geometry winner ("hybrid") then got one real training arm — and failed: val@50 of 0.3730. The basis that maximized update-energy capture anti-converted, because the vLLM rollouts run uncompressed, so a basis that sacrifices activation fidelity for update energy destroys train-vs-rollout consistency (research/runs/EXP-26/stepC_screen_report.md). H2's strong form is dead.
  3. Step B — the new merger. The successor "ef_powersgd" merger re-injects the energy the projection dropped, with no sign term at all. Arms: error-feedback, plain compression, and a dense reference. This produced the headline result.
  4. Step E — measure the bytes (no extra runs). Byte counters wired into the Step B/C runs measured the actual boundary traffic: a ratio of 0.0506, roughly a 19.8× reduction, read straight from the live training runs.

The new merger's idea is worth stating plainly. Projecting onto the rank-77 basis throws away whatever component of the gradient lives outside that subspace. Rather than guess at a correction, the error-feedback merger keeps a running, clipped, detached buffer of exactly that dropped residual and adds it back next time, in the same direction as the compressed update:

$$ e_t = \operatorname{clip}\!\big(\, \texttt{ef\_decay}\cdot e_{t-1} + (M - P_{G_{\text{comp}}}(M)),\ \ \texttt{ef\_clip}\cdot\lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert \big), \qquad G_{\text{corr}} = G_{\text{comp}} + e_t $$

Here \(M\) is the anchor's gradient average and \(P_{G_{\text{comp}}}(M)\) is the part of it already captured by the compressed update, so \(M - P_{G_{\text{comp}}}(M)\) is the off-subspace energy the projection missed; \(e_t\) accumulates that residual, clipped relative to the compressed gradient's norm and decayed by ef_decay. Crucially there is no \(\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)\) anywhere — the correction adds energy in the existing direction instead of flipping it. The limiting case (zero clip, zero decay) is bit-identical to plain compression, which the code enforces as a test (verl/workers/comm_eff/spectral_filter.py).

The guardrails that made the numbers trustworthy

None of this would mean anything if the diagnostic quietly cheated. Two classes of gate bound every stage. The realism guardrails kept the experiment honest about the decentralized target: the anchor is the only thing that updates \(Q\) and the fast path can only read it; the only full uncompressed pass lives inside the anchor; the anchor staleness is mandatory (never zero on the training path); and there is no "clean step" that secretly transmits a full-rank gradient. The correctness gates, probed before any GPU-hours were spent on a sweep, proved the instrumentation was inert: off-path parity (the capture hooks change no numbers), the measurement-only probes never feed the optimizer (verified bit-for-bit on the optimizer state), and the dumped tensors are genuinely fp32 (reconstruction error matched the logged scalar to within \(4.5\times10^{-5}\)). Every one of these was a hard gate: a failure aborts before the expensive work.

EXP-25: What We Achieved, and the Mathematics of the EMA-Sign Merger

EXP-25 has two stories. The first is a genuine win: it built and verified the realistic two-circuit substrate that every later experiment now runs on — a continuously-maintained, deliberately-stale, full-coverage uncompressed reference that drives the compression. The second is an honest falsification: the specific way EXP-25 chose to use that reference — borrowing its per-coordinate signs (the signed_ema merger) — cannot work, and we can now show exactly why with the actual measured numbers. This tab covers both: what was banked, and the small piece of mathematics that explains why stealing the anchor's signs is a coin-flip dressed up as a correction.

Part 1 — What EXP-25 actually achieved

The headline result is a STOP, but a STOP that left behind a working substrate and a sharp mechanistic picture. Four concrete things were banked.

1. The anchor circuit became the proven, realistic base. The slow uncompressed reference is no longer a wish — it was verified on-box. Its gradient covers all 196 weight matrices of the network (every layer, not a sampled handful), it is averaged correctly across data-parallel ranks (a mean, not a sum — a subtle bug that would have inflated it fourfold was checked for and ruled out), and that averaged gradient is bit-identical across ranks. The basis \(Q\) used by the fast compressed path is computed only by the anchor and broadcast out, with the fast path verified fail-closed — it can read \(Q\) but never write it (powersgd_basis_updates=0 throughout). This replaced the earlier, unrealistic "fresh clean step," which cheated by shipping a full uncompressed gradient on demand. The substrate is the durable contribution: every later experiment runs on it.

2. A clean, monotonic dose-response on the merger knob. The merger blends the fast gradient with the anchor's sign through one knob \(\alpha\); EXP-25 swept it across three byte-identical arms. The result is unambiguous and reproduced in Part 2's table: the more the stale sign is used, the worse training gets. There is no interior sweet spot.

3. The mechanism discovery — length-hacking, not low entropy, is the killer. When the merger drives training off a cliff, the symptom is not subtle. The model's answers explode in length toward the 16,000-token cap, while its entropy (a measure of how spread-out its next-token choices are, in nats — high means uncertain, low means confident) collapses toward zero. The instinct is to blame the entropy collapse. EXP-25 disproved that: the uncompressed dense control trains down to entropy 0.12 — lower than any surviving compressed arm — and stays healthy with bounded ~280-token answers. Low entropy is a confident solver, not a broken one. What actually destroys validation accuracy is the response-length runaway: the model discovers that emitting longer and longer text raises its odds of stumbling onto the answer string under the lenient reward, reward briefly rises, then the answers blow past usefulness and accuracy falls off a cliff.

4. A standing collapse watch. Because the failure is visible early — entropy halved by step 10, well before the run finishes — EXP-25 produced a reusable monitor applied to every run now: track entropy level and slope, response-length and truncation rate, and reward peak-then-degrade, and fire an alert the moment they co-occur. The collapse no longer has to be discovered the expensive way.

The KL guardrail diagnostic. To prove length-hacking is the proximate killer, EXP-25 re-ran the catastrophic \(\alpha=0\) arm with a small KL penalty added (a leash tying the policy to a fixed reference, which starves runaway non-stopping generations). The length explosion vanished — answers stayed at ~120 tokens, truncation stayed at zero — and the catastrophe (validation accuracy 0.354) became a mild decline to 0.679. The leash removed the symptom. But 0.679 is still below the no-help floor of 0.6914: the merger's underlying direction damage survives the guardrail. The brake stops the crash; it does not make the sign-borrowing helpful.

Part 2 — The mathematics of the EMA-sign bet

The merger combines the fast circuit's compressed gradient \(G_{\text{comp}}\) (its magnitude tells you how hard to push each weight) with the anchor's gradient average \(M\) (its sign tells you which way), one coordinate at a time, just before the optimizer:

$$ G_{\text{corr}} \;=\; \alpha\,G_{\text{comp}} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert \odot \operatorname{sign}(M), $$

where \(\lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert\) is the per-coordinate absolute value (the magnitudes), \(\operatorname{sign}(M)\) is \(+1\) or \(-1\) per coordinate (the directions from the anchor), \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication, and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) is the mixing knob. \(M\) is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the stale anchor gradient — a running blend \(M_t = \beta M_{t-1} + (1-\beta)G_{\text{anchor}}\) with \(\beta=0.95\), so it remembers roughly the last twenty refreshes and smooths out noise.

Why it could plausibly work

The bet rests on a real and respectable idea from distributed optimization called signSGD: when many workers each compute a noisy gradient, you can transmit just the sign of each coordinate (one bit instead of a full number) and take a majority vote. This works surprisingly well because the sign of an averaged gradient is scale-free and robust — compression noise mostly corrupts magnitudes, not which way a weight should move, and sign-based methods with a majority vote have proven convergence under reasonable assumptions.

Apply that intuition here. The anchor's \(M\) is an averaged, clean, uncompressed gradient — exactly the kind of robust sign source signSGD wants. If the direction the true update wants to go is stable over the five-tick refresh window, then a slightly stale sign is still the right sign. The merger would then give the best of both worlds: keep the fast circuit's well-calibrated magnitudes (\(\lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert\)), and borrow trustworthy direction bits from the clean reference (\(\operatorname{sign}(M)\)). Superficially, this is exactly the seam where a stale clean reference should help a noisy fast one.

Why it cannot work — the falsification chain

The whole argument hinges on one assumption: that the anchor's sign and the fast gradient's sign usually agree. They do not. We measured it.

(a) Sign agreement is a coin-flip. The fraction of coordinates (weighted by gradient magnitude) on which \(\operatorname{sign}(M)\) agrees with \(\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp}})\) was measured directly on real 32-bit tensors:

All sit at roughly one-half — a coin-flip. Crucially this holds even at zero staleness (a perfectly fresh anchor gradient), which kills the obvious defense. The disagreement is not caused by the sign being out of date; it is structural. For about half the coordinates, the merger flips the sign of the update.

(b) Why it is structural. Two reasons, both about the gradients being different objects rather than stale copies of one object. First, a loss mismatch: the anchor computes a clean policy-gradient loss (no clipping), while the fast path computes the real clipped PPO loss — different functions, different gradients. Second, and deeper, a near-orthogonality geometry. The true GRPO gradient on any single coordinate is a sum of signed per-sample terms that largely cancel, leaving most coordinates sitting near zero, where the sign is essentially decided by residual sampling noise. When you compare two independent noisy estimates of a quantity that is near zero on most coordinates, their per-coordinate signs agree about half the time — even when the two gradients' overall directions are aligned. This is the same geometry that makes two random high-dimensional vectors nearly orthogonal. The coin-flip is the expected signature, not an anomaly.

There was a further problem with the anchor \(M\) measured here, found later by a code review: it computed its gradient at the stale weights but on the current policy's trajectories (a stale-weights × current-batch mismatch, since the anchor was fed the current tick's batch). EXP-29 fixed this with on-policy replay — the anchor now grades the trajectories its own stale weights generated (see the EXP-29 tab). The important part for this tab: EXP-30 re-measured sign-agreement on the corrected, generator-consistent anchor and found it just as orthogonal (\(\cos \approx 0\)). So the coin-flip above is genuinely structural — not an artifact of the confound. The fix unlocked the residual route to SOTA (EXP-30) without un-falsifying the sign-replacement route analyzed here; both readings stand together.

(c) The confound-free measurement of the damage. The cleanest single number sidesteps every loss-and-operand subtlety by comparing the merger's output to its own input. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors (1 = identical direction, 0 = perpendicular). At \(\alpha=0.5\),

$$ \cos\!\big(G_{\text{comp}},\,G_{\text{corr}}\big) \;=\; 0.717 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = \arccos(0.717) \approx 44^{\circ}. $$

The merger rotates the already-compressed update by about 44 degrees — nearly halfway to perpendicular. It is not a gentle correction; it is a large reorientation of the step the optimizer takes.

(d) A sign flip with preserved magnitude is a large perturbation — the \(\sqrt{2}\) factor. Here is the core derivation. On a coordinate \(i\) where the anchor sign agrees with the fast sign, the merger leaves it untouched: \(G_{\text{corr},i} = G_{\text{comp},i}\). On a coordinate where they disagree, write \(G_{\text{comp},i} = \lvert g_i\rvert\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i})\) and \(\operatorname{sign}(M_i) = -\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i})\). Then

$$ G_{\text{corr},i} = \alpha\,\lvert g_i\rvert\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i}) + (1-\alpha)\,\lvert g_i\rvert\big(-\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i})\big) = (2\alpha-1)\,G_{\text{comp},i}. $$

So the entire effect of the merger lives on the disagreeing coordinates, scaled by the factor \((2\alpha-1)\): at \(\alpha=0\) the coordinate is fully reversed at full magnitude (a step in exactly the wrong direction), at \(\alpha=0.5\) it is zeroed, and at \(\alpha=1\) the merger is off. Now measure the size of the perturbation at \(\alpha=0\): a disagreeing coordinate contributes \(G_{\text{corr},i}-G_{\text{comp},i} = -2G_{\text{comp},i}\), so

$$ \big\lVert G_{\text{corr}} - G_{\text{comp}}\big\rVert^2 = \sum_{\text{disagree}} 4\,g_i^2 = 4 f \,\lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\lVert G_{\text{corr}}-G_{\text{comp}}\rVert}{\lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert} = 2\sqrt{f}, $$

where \(f\) is the magnitude-weighted fraction of disagreeing coordinates. EXP-25 logged this relative-change quantity every step: its median is 1.416 \(\approx \sqrt{2}\). Inverting, \(2\sqrt{f}=\sqrt{2}\) gives \(f = \tfrac12\) — confirming, from a completely independent measurement during the actual run, that half the gradient's mass has its sign flipped at full magnitude every single step.

(e) The dose-response is exactly what this model predicts. If the sign term injects a wrong-direction perturbation on half the coordinates, then dialing it up (lowering \(\alpha\)) should monotonically worsen training — which is precisely what was observed:

(f) The sharpening spiral. The chain closes mechanically. EXP-26 — The Moves, the Mathematics, the Geometry (and the Papers)

EXP-26 stopped guessing. Instead of inventing a merger and running an expensive training arm to see if it helped, we dumped the real tensors — the activations, the gradients, the basis — to fp32 and measured the geometry directly. This tab is the geometry made plain: what a subspace is, what our rank-77 basis actually captures, and why a basis can hold 99.85% of the picture a layer sends yet only 31.8% of the learning signal. Then the three things we tested on that geometry — a new basis content (Step C), error-feedback (Step B) — and what they taught us. The headline: the issue lands at REVISE, with the best realistic comm-efficient result to date (val@50 0.7210) two points short of parity, and one clean, decisive falsification along the way.

1. The geometry primer — subspaces as camera angles

Everything that flows across a pipeline boundary in this model is a vector in a 1536-dimensional space (the hidden size \(H=1536\) of Qwen2.5-1.5B). That is too many dimensions to picture, so use a smaller analogy and scale it up. Imagine a cloud of points in ordinary 3D space. A subspace is a flat slice through that cloud — a 2D plane, say. If you set up a camera pointed at that plane, the photo it takes is a projection: the 3D world flattened onto a 2D image. The camera keeps everything that lies in its plane and throws away everything sticking out toward or away from the lens.

A rank-77 orthonormal basis \(Q\) is exactly that camera, but pointed at a 77-dimensional slice of the 1536-dimensional space. "Orthonormal" means its 77 viewing directions are mutually perpendicular and unit-length — no direction is redundant or stretched. We write \(Q\) as a tall thin matrix, \(1536\times 77\): 77 columns, each a unit direction in the big space. The projection operator

$$ P_Q = Q\,Q^{\top} $$

is the verb "take the photo." Applied to any vector \(x\), the result \(P_Q x\) is "the part of \(x\) the camera can see" — \(x\) flattened onto the 77-dimensional slice. The part it can't see, \(x - P_Q x\), is what got thrown away. This is the whole compression trick: instead of shipping a full 1536-number activation across the pipeline boundary, we ship its 77 coordinates inside \(Q\) (that is the \(\hat A = (AQ)Q^{\top}\) you saw in earlier tabs), a ~20× saving.

Energy capture ratio — the single most important number in this tab. Ask "what fraction of the picture did the photo keep?" Measure it as the squared length retained: $$ \text{capture}(X) = \frac{\lVert P_Q X\rVert^2}{\lVert X\rVert^2} \in [0,1]. $$ A ratio of 1 means the camera saw everything (\(X\) already lay in the slice); a ratio of 0 means \(X\) pointed entirely out of frame. \(\lVert\cdot\rVert\) is just the length (Euclidean norm); squaring it makes it an energy, so the ratio is the fraction of total energy the basis spans.

Two more words. The principal directions of a cloud are the few axes along which it spreads the most — where the points usually sit. Off-principal directions are the rest, where there is little spread. A good rank-77 camera is aimed at the principal directions, because that is where most of the energy lives. Finally, cosine similarity measures whether two vectors point the same way: \(\cos(u,v)=\tfrac{u\cdot v}{\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert}\). It is 1 when they are identical in direction, 0 when they are perpendicular (unrelated), and negative when they oppose. A cosine of 0.717 corresponds to about a 44° angle; 0.956 is about 17°. We will lean on this number constantly, because the whole EXP-26 thesis is that the failure of EXP-25 was a direction problem, not a magnitude problem.

2. The central surprise — 99.85% of the picture, 31.8% of the lesson

Our basis \(Q_{\text{act}}\) is built from activations: it is aimed at the directions where the layer's outputs usually sit (block power iteration on the activation second moment — Tab 3's machinery). Step A measured two capture ratios for the same basis on the same real tensors:

  • Activation capture \(\lVert P_Q A\rVert^2/\lVert A\rVert^2 =\) 0.9985 — the camera keeps 99.85% of the picture the layer sends. Reconstruction is essentially lossless.
  • Update (gradient) capture \(\lVert P_Q G\rVert^2/\lVert G\rVert^2 =\) 0.318 — the same camera keeps only 31.8% of the learning signal. The off-principal share — the part of the update the basis cannot see — is 0.682, more than two-thirds.
The security-camera analogy. Picture a camera bolted above a shop doorway, aimed at the spot where customers usually stand to pay. For watching the queue it is perfect — it sees 99.85% of where people are. But the interesting thing right now is where people are moving — toward the back shelves, out the side exit — and that motion happens largely outside the frame. The activation second moment (where the layer's outputs sit) and the GRPO update (the direction the policy wants to change) live in different subspaces. A basis tuned to reconstruct activations is the camera aimed at where people stand; the learning signal is where they are moving. This is not an artifact — it is a real property of RLVR training, and a published one (see the papers section: RLVR provably learns off the principal directions).

3. What Step A actually measured — and the measurement craft

Step A dumped seven real fp32 tensors per target (the activation \(A\), its reconstruction \(\hat A\), the basis \(Q\), the compressed gradient \(G_{\text{comp}}\), the merger-corrected gradient \(G_{\text{corr}}\), the anchor's gradient EMA \(M\), and a fresh full uncompressed anchor gradient \(G_{\text{fresh}}\) at \(\text{delay\_K}=0\)) and computed the discriminators. Dump fidelity was tight: reconstruction drift 4.5×10⁻⁵, far below the \(10^{-3}\) gate — these are the real tensors, not a bf16 readout.

The craft worth understanding is the confound. The plan's first instinct was to compute \(\cos(G_{\text{dense}}, G_{\text{comp}})\) — does compression rotate the update away from dense? But that comparison is polluted two ways. First, a loss mismatch: the anchor computes a clean policy-gradient loss (ratio ≡ 1, no clip), while the fast path computes the real PPO-clip loss; once the policy drifts post-warmup the two gradients diverge for reasons that have nothing to do with compression. Second, an operand mismatch: PowerSGD compresses the activation gradient, not the weight gradient, so \(G_{\text{comp}}\) is simply not \(Q Q^{\top} G_{\text{weight}}\). Both push the naive cosine toward zero independent of direction (the measured \(\cos(G_{\text{fresh}}, G_{\text{comp}})=0.010\) is an artifact — plain PowerSGD r77 ties dense at 0.7415, so it cannot truly be orthogonal to dense).

The fix was to pivot to confound-free discriminators that never reference a dense gradient: compare the merger against its own input, \(\cos(G_{\text{comp}}, G_{\text{corr}})\), and rank families against the same reference so the constant confound cancels. On that clean footing:

4. Step C — the Q-content mathematics, and a beautiful falsification

If \(Q_{\text{act}}\) misses the learning signal, build a basis aimed at it instead. The elegant trick: every candidate basis differs only in what statistic feeds the power-iteration sketch \(V\) inside the anchor, so one short run can build all six candidates passively — accumulated under no_grad in the anchor's stale-weight pass, never touching the live \(Q\), the fast path, or the optimizer. Six geometry arms collapse into one cheap cell. A basis reshaped toward update energy at the expense of activation reconstruction lets the train-path policy drift away from the rollout policy — and on-policy RL punishes that mismatch immediately (the KL blows up). Training is a closed loop, not a static approximation problem: a geometry that "captures more update energy" on paper is worthless if it breaks the train/rollout contract. Step C is empirically falsified, cleanly. Honest footnote: even the geometric "win" was modest — the pure grad families (grad/tail) captured only ~5% of the gradient, because the boundary activation gradient is essentially rank-diffuse, so no 77-dimensional subspace captures it. There was never a dramatic edge to convert.

5. Step B — error-feedback mathematics (the result that survived)

Step B keeps the activation basis but fixes the merger. The idea of error feedback is to re-inject what compression dropped rather than override the direction. Concretely, per matrix (all fp32, detached):

$$ e_t = \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\underbrace{\text{decay}\cdot e_{t-1}}_{\text{remembered residual}} + \underbrace{(M - P(M))}_{\text{off-subspace part of the stale anchor EMA}}, \ \text{clip}\cdot \lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert\Big), \qquad G_{\text{corr}} = G_{\text{comp}} + e_t . $$

In words: \(M\) is the stale anchor's gradient EMA; \(P(M)\) is the part of it that \(G_{\text{comp}}\) already spans; the difference \(M-P(M)\) is the off-subspace residual — the energy compression dropped. We accumulate it with a decay (a leaky memory), cap its norm relative to \(\lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert\) so it can never dominate, and add it to \(G_{\text{comp}}\). Crucially there is no sign term: we only add dropped energy, so \(G_{\text{corr}}\) keeps \(G_{\text{comp}}\)'s direction. The limiting case is a built-in correctness check: with \(\text{decay}=\text{clip}=0\) the residual is floored to zero and \(G_{\text{corr}}\equiv G_{\text{comp}}\) — bit-identical to plain PowerSGD.

An honest difference from classical error feedback. In the original PowerSGD (Vogels et al.) and the Seide/Stich line of work, error feedback accumulates your own compression residual — the exact part of this gradient you failed to send, re-added next step — because a parameter-gradient residual has a stable home: the same parameter slot recurs every step. Our boundary activations do not have a stable slot (each residual is tied to a specific microbatch, trajectory, token, and weight instance, which is why the local reference notes argue activation EF is unsafe in GRPO). So we do not re-inject the activation residual directly; instead the residual proxy is the anchor-EMA's off-subspace component, a weight-gradient quantity that does persist across steps. It is error-feedback in spirit, on a different operand — we state this plainly rather than claim the classical guarantee.

The geometry confirmed the design. The direction-preservation discriminator \(\cos(G_{\text{comp}}, G_{\text{corr}})\) came in at 0.956 (≈17°) versus signed_ema's 0.717 (≈44°) — the merger now nudges rather than swings the update. The residual dose \(\lVert G_{\text{corr}}-G_{\text{comp}}\rVert/\lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert\) sat at a median 0.32, rising to 0.47 as the basis went stale (a real, growing correction, not a no-op). The outcome: val@50 0.7210, the best realistic comm-efficient result to date — +1.4 points over the falsified EXP-25 signed_ema, +7.7 points over plain PowerSGD on the same realistic substrate, with no deterministic collapse on the promoted run, and a measured ~19.8× communication reduction.

The catch. Parity is 0.7414; we landed 2.0 points short — hence REVISE, not PASS. And error feedback at \(\text{clip}=1.0\) showed a stochastic length-explosion ignition: of two identical-config realizations, one fired the EXP-25 length-hack fingerprint and OOM'd at step 42 (the ignition was dose-correlated), the other completed clean. The next iteration damps the residual (clip 1.0→0.5, decay 0.9→0.5) and extends to 100 steps — capping the dropped-energy kick that correlates with the ignition while keeping the direction-preserving correction that bought +7.7 points.

6. The papers we read and used

PowerSGD — Vogels, Karimireddy, Jaggi, arXiv:1905.13727, "Practical Low-Rank Gradient Compression for Distributed Optimization." The source of our compressor: power iteration to find a low-rank sketch of a gradient matrix, shipping \(r(m+n)\) numbers instead of \(mn\). It is also where the importance of error feedback comes from — re-injecting the compression residual is what lets aggressive low-rank compression match uncompressed SGD. Our ef_powersgd adapts that idea to the activation/anchor setting (see Step B's honest caveat above).

The Path Not Taken: RLVR Provably Learns Off the Principals — Zhu et al., arXiv:2511.08567. The most directly relevant paper: it argues (with a "Three-Gate" theory) that RLVR updates concentrate in off-principal parameter directions while leaving the principal spectrum stable. This is the published mirror of our central measurement — an activation-principal basis captures 99.85% of the picture but only 31.8% of the update, because the update genuinely lives off-principal.

RL Finetunes Small Subnetworks — Mukherjee et al., arXiv:2505.11711. Shows RL gains come from updating only 5–30% of parameters, and that training that subnetwork alone nearly recovers full finetuning. Motivation for the sparse/axis-aligned ticket family (a coordinate subnetwork rather than a dense subspace).

The Multiple Ticket Hypothesis: Random Sparse Subnetworks Suffice for RLVR — Adewuyi et al., arXiv:2602.01599. Finds that even random 1% sparse masks match full-model RLVR, and different masks barely overlap yet all succeed. Directly names our ticket construction and warns why a single hand-picked coordinate set has no special claim — consistent with ticket winning the geometry but carrying a forward-fidelity hazard (\(AC=0.293\)).

RL's Razor: Why Online RL Forgets Less — Shenfeld, Pari, Agrawal, arXiv:2509.04259. Argues on-policy RL implicitly favors solutions with minimal KL from the base policy. This is the theoretical backdrop for our Step C falsification: a basis that drives the train policy away from the (uncompressed) rollout policy fights the very KL-minimizing pressure that makes on-policy RL stable, and the train↔rollout KL explosion is exactly that fight.

The Invisible Leash: Why RLVR May or May Not Escape Its Origin — Wu et al., arXiv:2507.14843. RLVR sharpens precision but can shrink exploration/support. A caution for our parity-vs-surpass framing: greedy val gains need not mean expanded capability, so we treat parity (not surpass) as the realistic ceiling.

Does RL Really Incentivize Reasoning Beyond the Base Model? — Yue et al., arXiv:2504.13837. Using pass@k, finds current RLVR extracts rather than creates reasoning (base models can win at large \(k\)). Reinforces why we do not claim a dense-surpass from greedy mean@1 and defer pass@k / answer-entropy until parity is actually recovered.

Mechanism: not true error feedback — a persistent tangential carrier

The implemented update (per matrix, per tick) is G_corr = G_t + e_t where e_t = δ·e_{t−1} + clip(comp_t) and comp_t = M_t − ⟨G_t,M_t⟩/‖G_t‖² · G_t. Three structural facts determine everything:

  1. comp_t ⊥ G_t exactly (by construction: spectral_filter.py:378–379). Every injected force is tangential — it neither helps nor hurts descent, but pushes sideways.
  2. M has ~50-global-step memory (\(\beta=0.95\), refresh every 2.5 global steps). The tangential push points in a direction that is effectively constant over the run length.
  3. The projection is nearly vacuous: \(\cos(G_{\text{anchor}}, G_{\text{comp}}) \approx 0.01\)–\(0.06\) (Step-A, EXP-26), so comp_t ≈ M — this is direct injection of the stale EMA, not residual correction.

A persistent tangential force on a reward-flat "correct-but-longer" manifold integrates linearly over time: \(\|\Sigma e_t\| \sim \lambda \cdot T \cdot \|G\|\). There is no telescoping cancellation (unlike classic Stich/Karimireddy error feedback), so the cumulative transport grows with run length. Dose magnitude controls only the lag, not whether ignition occurs.

Damping = delay, not prevention. EF dose ratio ef r1/EXP-27 = 0.200/0.092 = 2.17×; lock-in step ratio = s30/s61 = 2.03× — near-exactly linear. EXP-27 ignited at its dose minimum (~0.02, steps 45–67). Cutting clip/decay further would extend the lag further but cannot prevent transport along the reward-flat direction. Every per-step health metric (cos(G,G+e) ≥ 0.982, score 0.73–0.84) was correct and certifies only the descent component — it says nothing about the tangential one. EXP-29: The Trajectory Fix — Make the Anchor Grade Its Own Homework

Every result up to this point rested on a quiet assumption that turned out to be wrong. The anchor circuit's whole job is to compute a clean, trustworthy gradient \(M\) from a stale snapshot of the weights — but it was computing that gradient on the wrong data. It graded the current policy's answers using old weights, which is neither the old policy's gradient nor the new one's. EXP-29 (issue #29, “Correct the anchor gradients trap utilisation”, operator-directed after a code review) fixes this: the anchor now replays the exact trajectories its stale weights actually generated. No training-curve headline — this is an infrastructure correctness fix — but it is the fix that made the later SOTA legitimate.

What the anchor is supposed to compute

Recall the two-circuit design. The fast compressed circuit runs every step; the anchor circuit fires every few ticks, loading a delay_K = 5-tick-stale snapshot of the weights into a clean, no-compression clone and running one full forward/backward to produce the true gradient average \(M\). That \(M\) is the program's reference signal — the merger folds it into the noisy compressed gradient, and it drives the projection basis \(Q\). If \(M\) is contaminated, everything downstream inherits the contamination.

The anchor uses a deliberately clean loss (anchor_pg_loss, verl/workers/comm_eff/anchor.py): a plain REINFORCE policy gradient with the importance ratio held at \(1\) (no PPO clipping, old_log_probs deliberately ignored), so its gradient is exactly

$$ G_{\text{anchor}} \;=\; \nabla_\theta\big[\!-\!A \cdot \log\pi_\theta\big]\Big|_{\theta = \theta_{t-K}} \;=\; -\,A\cdot\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta_{t-K}}. $$

This is the textbook “clean step's gradient, evaluated at the stale weights.” The advantages \(A\) and the response tokens are read straight off the batch. And there is the trap: which batch?

The bug: the anchor graded the current policy's trajectories

On the training path, GRPO is on-policy — for each step \(t\), vLLM generates rollouts using the current weights (\(\theta_{t-1}\)), and the advantages \(A\) are computed from those rollouts' rewards. The old anchor refresh loaded the stale weights \(\theta_{t-K}\) into the clone but then forwarded anchor_data = data.copy() — the current tick's batch (transformer_impl.py). So it computed

$$ G_{\text{anchor}}^{\text{(old)}} \;=\; -\,A_{\text{current}}\cdot\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{t-K}}, $$

with no importance correction between the policy that generated the data (\(\pi_{\text{current}}\)) and the weights doing the scoring (\(\theta_{t-K}\)). This object is neither the stale policy's true gradient (that would need the stale policy's own trajectories) nor the current policy's (that would need the current weights). It is a stale-weights × current-batch confound — a mismatch baked into \(M\)'s content across all of EXP-25, EXP-26, and EXP-27.

The analogy. Picture the careful clerk again, the one who keeps the only fully-correct copy of the ledger. The clerk is meant to redo this morning's transactions using the rules as they stood a week ago, to measure how much the rules have drifted. Instead, the clerk was redoing today's brand-new transactions with last week's rulebook — an answer to a question nobody asked. The drift measurement is contaminated by the fact that the transactions themselves are not the ones last week's rules would have produced.

This was a deliberate original design choice, not a coding slip — “the clean step's gradient at the stale weights” was taken to mean “clean loss, stale weights, whatever batch is in hand.” But for the decentralized-pipeline target the right object is unambiguous: a slow, out-of-date worker computes gradients on the trajectories it itself generated from its own weights. The confound had to go before any claim about how to use \(M\) could be trusted.

The fix: on-policy replay

The correction (anchor.replay_paired_batch = true) is conceptually simple and costs no extra generation. A fire-aware ring buffer retains recent mini-batches — the tokens and their advantages — each tagged with the weights that produced it; when the anchor fires, it replays the paired batch instead of the current one (anchor_data = _replay_batch.copy(), transformer_impl.py):

$$ G_{\text{anchor}}^{\text{(fixed)}} \;=\; -\,A_{t-K}\cdot\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{t-K}} \qquad\text{on the batch } \theta_{t-K}\text{ generated.} $$

Now the weights and the trajectories match: a self-consistent on-policy gradient of the stale snapshot. The alignment is exact, not approximate — the weight snapshot taken at the first training tick of a global step is precisely the snapshot vLLM used to generate that step's rollouts, so storing one snapshot per global step plus the per-tick batches yields exact (batch, generator-weights) pairs (warmup included). Data staleness is exactly \(K\) ticks; the weights are always the batch's true generator (realized weight staleness \(K\) or \(K{+}1\) on the two-ticks-per-step substrate, logged and hard-asserted never fresher than \(K\)).

A companion knob, anchor.snapshot_device = cpu, moves the delay_K + 1 full weight snapshots (~18.6 GB if kept on the GPU) into CPU RAM, loaded to the device only inside the refresh. The batches were already CPU-resident, so the new ring costs essentially zero extra GPU memory — an explicit operator requirement (the fixed substrate must not grow its HBM footprint). Both knobs default OFF (replay_paired_batch = false, snapshot_device = gpu): with the fix disabled, not a single existing line changes and the run is byte-identical to before.

Why it mattered — and, honestly, what it did not fix

The enabling consequence. With a self-consistent anchor, the residual \(\delta = G_{\text{anchor}}^{\text{rep}} - G_{\text{comp}}\), measured at identical batch and weights, becomes a clean measurement of one thing only: the error the codec introduced. The relevance probe's noise floor (~0.01 nats) is the same band EXP-30 later used to certify that \(\delta\) is codec error and not an objective/data mismatch. That clean \(\delta\) is exactly the quantity the SOTA merger (EXP-30's delayed_ef, λ=1) re-injects — so on-policy replay is the precondition that made the next experiment's parity result legitimate rather than confounded.
The honest non-result. Making \(M\) valid did not rescue the old sign / blend idea. When EXP-30 measured it, the valid generator-consistent gradient was just as orthogonal to the live compressed gradient as the old confounded \(M\) had been (\(\cos \approx 0\) either way). So the EXP-25 “sign agreement is a coin-flip” finding was genuinely structural, not an artifact of the trajectory confound — fixing the data did not open up the blend geometry (which is why EXP-30's blend gate closed without a training run). The trajectory fix and the sign coin-flip are two independent facts: EXP-29 removed a real confound and unlocked the residual route to SOTA, but it did not un-falsify the sign-replacement route. Both readings stand together.

EXP-30: The δ-Residual Merger — Communication-Efficient GRPO Reaches Dense Parity

This is the experiment that produced the program's current state of the art. With a trustworthy anchor in hand (EXP-29), EXP-30 asked the sharpest possible version of the question: instead of borrowing the anchor's signs or its off-subspace energy, what if we feed back the exact difference between the clean gradient and the compressed gradient, measured on the very same data? That correction — the “delayed error-feedback” merger, B2 — trains GSM8K to val@50 ≈ 0.75, reaching the dense band at roughly 5% of the gradient-communication cost, with no length-explosion through 100 steps. It is the direct vindication of EXP-27's closing recommendation: rebuild error-feedback on the codec's own dropped residual, and the exogenous carrier that caused ignition disappears.

Diagnose-then-spend, once more — and a gate that closed

EXP-30 reused EXP-26's discipline: a cheap geometry probe decides which expensive merger earns a training run. A 20-step probe (stepA_geometry_probe) measured the anchor's valid, replayed gradient against the live compressed gradient on identical batches, and two pre-registered gates — thresholds fixed in advance — decided what ran next.

The blend gate closing is itself a result. Even with the now-valid anchor, the clean gradient stays essentially orthogonal to the compressed one (agreement \(\approx 0.01\)) — so a linear blend has almost nothing aligned to add, exactly as EXP-25/26 predicted. The gate retired the entire blend family for the cost of a 4-GPU-hour probe instead of a full training arm. The residual gate, by contrast, confirmed two things the residual route needs: \(\delta\) is the right size (norm ratio ~1) and it is genuine codec error, not an objective mismatch (loss-mismatch 0.01 nats, sitting in EXP-29's relevance band — the payoff of the trajectory fix).

The merger: feed back the exact codec residual

EXP-26's error-feedback re-injected the off-subspace projection of the anchor EMA — and because the projection was nearly vacuous, that reduced to injecting the stale EMA almost raw, an exogenous force that integrated into the tangential carrier and ignited (the EXP-27 story). B2 does something cleaner. Using the valid replayed anchor gradient \(G_{\text{anchor}}^{\text{rep}}\) and the compressed gradient \(G_{\text{comp}}\) on the identical (batch, weights) pair, it forms the exact residual and adds it back, at full strength, with no decay buffer and no sign term:

$$ \delta \;=\; G_{\text{anchor}}^{\text{rep}} \;-\; G_{\text{comp}}, \qquad G_{\text{corr}} \;=\; G_{\text{comp}} \;+\; \lambda\,\delta, \quad \lambda = 1,\ \beta_{\text{anc}} = 0. $$

At \(\lambda = 1\) this telescopes to \(G_{\text{corr}} = G_{\text{anchor}}^{\text{rep}}\): the compressed gradient is used as a transport for the clean one, and the codec error cancels exactly. Because \(\delta\) is the circuit's own dropped residual (endogenous), not an external EMA pushed in sideways, there is no persistent tangential force to integrate — which is precisely why it does not ignite. \(\beta_{\text{anc}} = 0\) keeps the anchor signal memoryless across fires (it is held between fires, not compounded), and the \(K\)-delay is the honest staleness of a slow reference.

It is three times too large and points almost entirely the wrong way. (This is the weight-space confirmation of EXP-26's activation-space finding that the basis captured only ~1/3 of the update energy.) A blend can only add a near-orthogonal partner and dilute; the residual \(\delta\) simultaneously cancels the stale codec artifact and injects the true direction. That is the whole mechanism.

The sub-basis probe: faster, but not a better optimum

Before the tournament, EXP-31 tested its most mechanistically-motivated surpass idea: a rank-2 sub-basis correction that adds back the off-principal gradient mode the act-basis \(Q\) misses (the stable-rank-2 object finding F3 had flagged). The lesson that set up the tournament. The sub-basis speeds the path to the optimum but does not find a better optimum. That is the recurring shape of every comm-eff correction the program has built: it can recover what compression lost and converge faster, but it converges to the same place dense does. To go past dense you need a signal dense itself lacks — which is exactly the hypothesis the four-lever tournament was designed to test and exhaust.

Why all four are null — the mechanistic close

You cannot beat dense by manipulating a stale reconstruction of dense. The correction \(\delta\) reconstructs the dense gradient evaluated on stale data. Re-weighting it (L3), accumulating it (L2), perturbing it (L4), or de-noising it (L1) all operate on that same stale estimate of the dense gradient — so each, at best, recovers dense and lands at parity, and at worst (L2 μ=0.9) just piles staleness on staleness. There is no interior optimum to tune toward, because the target the correction reconstructs is dense. To exceed dense, the comm-efficient path would have to inject a signal dense genuinely lacks — and no way of using the anchor produces such a signal.

What surpass would actually require (and why it is out of scope here)

The deep mechanism rules out the entire “manipulate δ” family. Every credible surpass route now lies outside the anchor-usage axis and outside this experiment's locked surface:

  • a generation-side diversity change (higher \(n\), rollout temperature) converting to a pass@k or relocated-mode edge — explicitly locked off here, since generation runs uncompressed and is sacred;
  • a fundamentally different decentralized-RL objective, not a re-use of the same stale dense estimate.

None is a non-blocked knob in EXP-31, so there is no admissible REVISE — a future attempt has to re-scope the locked surface with a new plan and an operator decision, not iterate a lever. That is what makes STOP the honest verdict: the anchor-usage axis is complete and exhausted, and the program is at defensible parity. Where that leaves us is the final tab.

What is settled

  • The substrate is realistic and mechanically correct. The anchor is the only thing that updates the projection basis \(Q\), the fast path can only read it, the only uncompressed pass lives inside the anchor, and there is no hidden full-rank “clean step.” Since EXP-29 the anchor also grades the trajectories its own stale weights generated — the gradient signal is now confound-free.
  • Parity is reached (within evaluation noise). EXP-31's apples-to-apples dense rerun put the bar at 0.7506; B2's 0.7400–0.7528 lineage sits inside the dense band. The “0.044 gap to dense” earlier experiments chased was a wrong-reference artifact.
  • The saving is real and measured. Boundary-activation traffic is cut ~19.8× on the fast path (byte ratio ~0.0505), ~4× once the anchor's own traffic is honestly amortized in — read straight off the live runs, not estimated.
  • It reproduces from one launcher. The exact B2 knob set is frozen as the canonical baseline (runs/EXP-31/B2_baseline/) that every later arm builds on.

What is closed or falsified

  • Sign-replacement (signed_ema) — the anchor's sign agrees with the compressed gradient only ~50% of the time, structurally, even when fresh and even when valid (EXP-25, re-confirmed on the valid anchor in EXP-30). Dead.
  • Update-energy / hybrid basis (Step C) — a basis tuned for gradient energy anti-converts because rollouts run uncompressed (EXP-26, val 0.373). Dead.
  • The M-fed exogenous-carrier merger class — any correction that injects a persistent stale direction integrates into a reward-flat length-explosion (EXP-27 ignition at step ~61). B2's endogenous residual avoids this; the carrier family is retired.
  • The entire anchor-usage axis for surpass — re-weighting, accumulating, perturbing, and de-noising the correction \(\delta\) all cap at parity, because \(\delta\) reconstructs the dense gradient on stale data and you cannot beat dense by manipulating a stale copy of dense (EXP-31, four levers, all null → STOP). Do not re-propose anchor-usage levers.

What is genuinely open — the only live to-dos

  1. Upgrade parity from “within noise” to “established” (cheap, bounded). Both the dense bar and B2 are single draws inside a ~±0.024 band. Seed replicates of both arms would pin the two bands and turn the statistical tie into a multi-seed result. This is the one measurement that closes the parity claim, and it needs no new code.
  2. Surpass requires re-scoping the locked surface — a new plan + operator decision, not a lever. The deep mechanism says a comm-efficient method can only exceed dense by injecting a signal dense itself lacks. None of the candidates is reachable on the current locked surface:
    • a different forward basis that captures the stable-rank-2 off-principal mode the act-basis \(Q\) misses (must avoid Step-C's update-energy trap);
    • a generation-side diversity change (higher \(n\), rollout temperature) converting to a pass@k or relocated-mode edge — this is the only compression-specific bet, and it changes the sacred uncompressed generation path;
    • a fundamentally different decentralized-RL objective.
    Each is an operator decision about the fixed control surface, not a researcher tuning step.
The honest bottom line. Communication-efficient GRPO matches dense at ~5% of the gradient-communication cost, on a realistic decentralized-pipeline substrate, reproducibly — the M6 milestone in everything but a multi-seed parity band. The surpass-dense thesis, as far as the anchor-usage axis can test it, is falsified: you cannot out-perform dense by being a cleverer copy of dense. Any next step toward surpassing is a deliberate change to the locked surface, made with eyes open, not another merger to tune.
