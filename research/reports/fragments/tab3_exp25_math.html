EXP-25: What We Achieved, and the Mathematics of the EMA-Sign Merger

EXP-25 has two stories. The first is a genuine win: it built and verified the realistic two-circuit substrate that every later experiment now runs on — a continuously-maintained, deliberately-stale, full-coverage uncompressed reference that drives the compression. The second was read at the time as a falsification: the specific way EXP-25 chose to use that reference — borrowing its per-coordinate signs (the signed_ema merger) — appeared not to work, and the mathematics below shows exactly why on the circuit as it then stood. That qualifier turned out to matter. A later fix (EXP-29) changed how the anchor computes its gradient, and on the corrected circuit the very same merger trains stably to near-parity (0.7271, EXP-32). So the honest reading today is not "cannot work" but works-but-dominated: the sign merger is fine, just beaten by error feedback (the default method). This tab keeps the original analysis — it correctly diagnoses the old circuit — and threads through, at each step, what the #29 fix changed.

[2026-06-16] Headline correction. The strong claim this tab originally made — that the sign merger is net-harmful at every \(\alpha\) — was an artifact of a broken circuit, not a property of the merger. It rested on (i) a wrong floor (0.7415, an old fresh-clean-step config) and (ii) an anchor that graded the current policy's trajectories with stale weights, producing an off-policy "wrong-trajectory" sign source. Fix the floor to the real one (0.6300, plain PowerSGD on the stale anchor, no merger) and fix the anchor's data (EXP-29), and signed_ema at \(\alpha=0.5\) trains stably to 0.7271 — well above the floor and within striking distance of dense (~0.75). It is dominated by error feedback (delayed_ef, ~0.74, the default), not harmful. Read the falsification chain below as a correct post-mortem of the pre-#29 circuit, now superseded.

Part 1 — What EXP-25 actually achieved

The headline result is a STOP, but a STOP that left behind a working substrate and a sharp mechanistic picture. Four concrete things were banked.

1. The anchor circuit became the proven, realistic base. The slow uncompressed reference is no longer a wish — it was verified on-box. Its gradient covers all 196 weight matrices of the network (every layer, not a sampled handful), it is averaged correctly across data-parallel ranks (a mean, not a sum — a subtle bug that would have inflated it fourfold was checked for and ruled out), and that averaged gradient is bit-identical across ranks. The basis \(Q\) used by the fast compressed path is computed only by the anchor and broadcast out, with the fast path verified fail-closed — it can read \(Q\) but never write it (powersgd_basis_updates=0 throughout). This replaced the earlier, unrealistic "fresh clean step," which cheated by shipping a full uncompressed gradient on demand. The substrate is the durable contribution: every later experiment runs on it.

2. A clean, monotonic dose-response on the merger knob. The merger blends the fast gradient with the anchor's sign through one knob \(\alpha\); EXP-25 swept it across three byte-identical arms. The result is unambiguous and reproduced in Part 2's table: the more the stale sign is used, the worse training gets. There is no interior sweet spot.

3. The mechanism discovery — length-hacking, not low entropy, is the killer. When the merger drives training off a cliff, the symptom is not subtle. The model's answers explode in length toward the 16,000-token cap, while its entropy (a measure of how spread-out its next-token choices are, in nats — high means uncertain, low means confident) collapses toward zero. The instinct is to blame the entropy collapse. EXP-25 disproved that: the uncompressed dense control trains down to entropy 0.12 — lower than any surviving compressed arm — and stays healthy with bounded ~280-token answers. Low entropy is a confident solver, not a broken one. What actually destroys validation accuracy is the response-length runaway: the model discovers that emitting longer and longer text raises its odds of stumbling onto the answer string under the lenient reward, reward briefly rises, then the answers blow past usefulness and accuracy falls off a cliff.

4. A standing collapse watch. Because the failure is visible early — entropy halved by step 10, well before the run finishes — EXP-25 produced a reusable monitor applied to every run now: track entropy level and slope, response-length and truncation rate, and reward peak-then-degrade, and fire an alert the moment they co-occur. The collapse no longer has to be discovered the expensive way.

The KL guardrail diagnostic. To prove length-hacking is the proximate killer, EXP-25 re-ran the catastrophic \(\alpha=0\) arm with a small KL penalty added (a leash tying the policy to a fixed reference, which starves runaway non-stopping generations). The length explosion vanished — answers stayed at ~120 tokens, truncation stayed at zero — and the catastrophe (validation accuracy 0.354) became a mild decline to 0.679. This pins length-hacking as the proximate mechanism of the \(\alpha=0\) collapse, which is the durable point. The original tab went further — reading 0.679 as "below the floor, so the sign damage survives the brake" — but that read used the wrong floor and the pre-#29 circuit; on the corrected circuit \(\alpha=0.5\) needs no leash at all and reaches 0.7271. So treat this as a clean diagnosis of the old \(\alpha=0\) failure, not as evidence the sign primitive is intrinsically harmful.

Part 2 — The mathematics of the EMA-sign bet

The merger combines the fast circuit's compressed gradient \(G_{\text{comp}}\) (its magnitude tells you how hard to push each weight) with the anchor's gradient average \(M\) (its sign tells you which way), one coordinate at a time, just before the optimizer:

$$ G_{\text{corr}} \;=\; \alpha\,G_{\text{comp}} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert \odot \operatorname{sign}(M), $$

where \(\lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert\) is the per-coordinate absolute value (the magnitudes), \(\operatorname{sign}(M)\) is \(+1\) or \(-1\) per coordinate (the directions from the anchor), \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication, and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) is the mixing knob. \(M\) is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the stale anchor gradient — a running blend \(M_t = \beta M_{t-1} + (1-\beta)G_{\text{anchor}}\). EXP-25 ran this with \(\beta=0.95\), smoothing over roughly the last twenty refreshes. That choice turns out to matter more than the sign rule: because RL policy gradients are valid only at the policy that produced them, accumulating old ones (\(\beta>0\)) folds in stale, off-policy directions, whereas using only the freshest anchor gradient (\(\beta_{\text{anc}}=0\), \(M=G_{\text{anchor}}\)) is what the corrected-circuit result relies on. That distinction is developed in the anchor tab; here we analyze the merger geometry EXP-25 actually measured.

Why it could plausibly work

The bet rests on a real and respectable idea from distributed optimization called signSGD: when many workers each compute a noisy gradient, you can transmit just the sign of each coordinate (one bit instead of a full number) and take a majority vote. This works surprisingly well because the sign of an averaged gradient is scale-free and robust — compression noise mostly corrupts magnitudes, not which way a weight should move, and sign-based methods with a majority vote have proven convergence under reasonable assumptions.

Apply that intuition here. The anchor's \(M\) is an averaged, clean, uncompressed gradient — exactly the kind of robust sign source signSGD wants. If the direction the true update wants to go is stable over the five-tick refresh window, then a slightly stale sign is still the right sign. The merger would then give the best of both worlds: keep the fast circuit's well-calibrated magnitudes (\(\lvert G_{\text{comp}}\rvert\)), and borrow trustworthy direction bits from the clean reference (\(\operatorname{sign}(M)\)). Superficially, this is exactly the seam where a stale clean reference should help a noisy fast one.

Why it didn't work on the old circuit — the falsification chain

The whole argument hinges on one assumption: that the anchor's sign and the fast gradient's sign usually agree. On the EXP-25 circuit they did not, and we measured it. (Hold onto why they disagreed — that turns out to be the fixable part, addressed at the end of this section.)

(a) Sign agreement is a coin-flip. The fraction of coordinates (weighted by gradient magnitude) on which \(\operatorname{sign}(M)\) agrees with \(\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp}})\) was measured directly on real 32-bit tensors:

Reference for the signStalenessSign agreement with \(G_{\text{comp}}\)
Anchor EMA \(M\) (plain PowerSGD arm)delay = 0 (fresh)0.500
Anchor EMA \(M\) (signed_ema arm)delay = 0 (fresh)0.523
Anchor EMA \(M\) (signed_ema arm)delay = 5 (stale)0.520

All sit at roughly one-half — a coin-flip. Crucially this holds even at zero staleness (a perfectly fresh anchor gradient), which kills the obvious defense. The disagreement is not caused by the sign being out of date; it is structural. For about half the coordinates, the merger flips the sign of the update.

(b) Why it is structural. Two reasons, both about the gradients being different objects rather than stale copies of one object. First, a loss mismatch: the anchor computes a clean policy-gradient loss (no clipping), while the fast path computes the real clipped PPO loss — different functions, different gradients. Second, and deeper, a near-orthogonality geometry. The true GRPO gradient on any single coordinate is a sum of signed per-sample terms that largely cancel, leaving most coordinates sitting near zero, where the sign is essentially decided by residual sampling noise. When you compare two independent noisy estimates of a quantity that is near zero on most coordinates, their per-coordinate signs agree about half the time — even when the two gradients' overall directions are aligned. This is the same geometry that makes two random high-dimensional vectors nearly orthogonal. The coin-flip is the expected signature, not an anomaly.

[2026-06-16] A third issue — the trajectory confound — and what survived it. There was a further problem with the anchor \(M\) measured here, found later by a code review: it computed its gradient at the stale weights but on the current policy's trajectories (a stale-weights × current-batch mismatch, since the anchor was fed the current tick's batch). EXP-29 fixed this with on-policy replay — the anchor now grades the trajectories its own stale weights generated (see the EXP-29 tab). The important part for this tab: EXP-30 re-measured sign-agreement on the corrected, generator-consistent anchor and found it just as orthogonal (\(\cos \approx 0\)). So the coin-flip above is genuinely structural — not an artifact of the confound. The fix unlocked the residual route to SOTA (EXP-30) without un-falsifying the sign-replacement route analyzed here; both readings stand together.

(c) The confound-free measurement of the damage. The cleanest single number sidesteps every loss-and-operand subtlety by comparing the merger's output to its own input. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors (1 = identical direction, 0 = perpendicular). At \(\alpha=0.5\),

$$ \cos\!\big(G_{\text{comp}},\,G_{\text{corr}}\big) \;=\; 0.717 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = \arccos(0.717) \approx 44^{\circ}. $$

The merger rotates the already-compressed update by about 44 degrees — nearly halfway to perpendicular. It is not a gentle correction; it is a large reorientation of the step the optimizer takes.

(d) A sign flip with preserved magnitude is a large perturbation — the \(\sqrt{2}\) factor. Here is the core derivation. On a coordinate \(i\) where the anchor sign agrees with the fast sign, the merger leaves it untouched: \(G_{\text{corr},i} = G_{\text{comp},i}\). On a coordinate where they disagree, write \(G_{\text{comp},i} = \lvert g_i\rvert\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i})\) and \(\operatorname{sign}(M_i) = -\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i})\). Then

$$ G_{\text{corr},i} = \alpha\,\lvert g_i\rvert\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i}) + (1-\alpha)\,\lvert g_i\rvert\big(-\operatorname{sign}(G_{\text{comp},i})\big) = (2\alpha-1)\,G_{\text{comp},i}. $$

So the entire effect of the merger lives on the disagreeing coordinates, scaled by the factor \((2\alpha-1)\): at \(\alpha=0\) the coordinate is fully reversed at full magnitude (a step in exactly the wrong direction), at \(\alpha=0.5\) it is zeroed, and at \(\alpha=1\) the merger is off. Now measure the size of the perturbation at \(\alpha=0\): a disagreeing coordinate contributes \(G_{\text{corr},i}-G_{\text{comp},i} = -2G_{\text{comp},i}\), so

$$ \big\lVert G_{\text{corr}} - G_{\text{comp}}\big\rVert^2 = \sum_{\text{disagree}} 4\,g_i^2 = 4 f \,\lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\lVert G_{\text{corr}}-G_{\text{comp}}\rVert}{\lVert G_{\text{comp}}\rVert} = 2\sqrt{f}, $$

where \(f\) is the magnitude-weighted fraction of disagreeing coordinates. EXP-25 logged this relative-change quantity every step: its median is 1.416 \(\approx \sqrt{2}\). Inverting, \(2\sqrt{f}=\sqrt{2}\) gives \(f = \tfrac12\) — confirming, from a completely independent measurement during the actual run, that half the gradient's mass has its sign flipped at full magnitude every single step.

(e) The dose-response is exactly what this model predicts. If the sign term injects a wrong-direction perturbation on half the coordinates, then dialing it up (lowering \(\alpha\)) should monotonically worsen training — which is precisely what was observed:

The best arm sits at the least-correction edge of the swept range, and \(\alpha\to1\) (no sign term at all — plain PowerSGD) is predicted to be best of all. The correction's optimum is to be turned off. That is a verdict on the primitive, not a tuning miss.

(f) The sharpening spiral. The chain closes mechanically. A persistent wrong-sign, full-magnitude force on half the coordinates is exactly magnitude-preserving signSGD pointed down a wrong axis: it destroys the natural per-coordinate cancellation that keeps GRPO steps modest, the gradient norm inflates (from the dense ~0.39 to ~3–11 at \(\alpha=0\)), the policy sharpens hard, entropy collapses, and — with no KL or entropy brake — the only reward-correlated escape is to emit ever-longer answers. Length explodes, reward peaks then crashes, validation falls off the cliff. The Part-1 mechanism and the Part-2 mathematics are the same story told forward and backward. [2026-06-11 refinement (EXP-27 post-mortem): the general ordering is now established as length-first — the spiral ignites and entropy collapse trails it (ef r1 ignited at entropy 0.83, then collapsed; the boundary is joint dose×sharpness, not an entropy line). The α=0 narrative above remains valid as the extreme-dose case where both move almost together. See the EXP-27 tab.]

The honest 2026-06-10 nuance (from EXP-26). A later experiment ran plain PowerSGD with no merger at all on this same realistic stale-anchor substrate and scored 0.6437 — below signed_ema's 0.7066 and below the frozen-basis floor of 0.6914. So on the realistic substrate, signed_ema was partially rescuing a substrate that was itself degrading (the stale-anchor basis refresh alone is a drag). EXP-25's "merger is net-harmful" verdict was measured against fresh, clean references — not against the realistic baseline. This reframes the bookkeeping: the merger was not the only thing dragging accuracy down. But it does not un-falsify the sign mechanism. The coin-flip sign agreement (0.50–0.52 even when fresh) and the 44-degree rotation are confound-free measurements of the sign term itself; they stand regardless of what the substrate around it was doing. The successor that removed the sign term entirely and instead fed back what compression drops (error-feedback PowerSGD) reached 0.7210 with the rotation cut to about 17 degrees — direct vindication that the sign-replacement, specifically, was the defect. [2026-06-16: that error-feedback line was itself later rebuilt on the codec's own exact residual (EXP-30's δ-residual merger) and reached dense parity, ~0.75 — the 0.7210 here is superseded; see the EXP-30 tab.]
