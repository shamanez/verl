What did we actually test? — telescope vs. astronomy

A fair question: if EXP-45 passed, what does the pass mean — and did we measure any real science? This experiment has two layers, and the PASS is staked on only the first one.

Layer 1 — the instrument (what PASS certifies)

We built and calibrated a measurement tool (moat_scorecard.py) and proved it trustworthy. That is all the 13 gates check: the arithmetic is exact (hold-stale reads identically 1.0), the metric functions are the shared ones with 0.0 parity error, all 338 matrices are classified exactly, and the schema round-trips on two machines. "Correctness & completeness" means the ruler is provably accurate with every marking — not that weight projection is a good idea.

Layer 2 — the measurements (real science-shaped data)

Pointed at a real GRPO run (160 fp32 snapshots of Qwen2.5-1.5B), the calibrated tool recorded genuine facts about how the weights move: does linear extrapolation beat reusing old weights (weight_proj_ratio), how straight is each matrix's path (traj_r2), how far ahead a forecast survives (h*). These are scientific measurements — EXP-45 simply does not stake its pass/fail on interpreting them.

So "not a science claim" means the verdict is about the instrument, not the phenomenon. Think telescope vs. astronomy — EXP-45 proves the telescope is in focus and every dial works; it does not publish the astronomy. But the telescope, pointed at the sky, did record real photons: at (Δ=20, h=40) naive linear extrapolation scores ratio 1.404 (worse than doing nothing at long range) yet h* = 5 (it wins out to ~5 ticks) — i.e. the weight trajectory is locally linear but globally curved.

Why split it this way? The project deliberately separates "is the measurement correct?" (#45) from "what does it mean?" (#47 linear/damped · #48 second-order · #49 self-correcting, rendered by #56). So when #56 concludes whether extrapolation helps, the conclusion rests on an instrument already proven correct rather than a possibly-buggy one. The naive_linear numbers below are a real-data smoke test that a non-trivial method flows through every code path — the interpretation is the lanes' job. The comparison to Wang et al. 2026 below reads those Layer-2 measurements against the published literature.

How to read the ratio

weight_proj_ratio = ‖θ̂(t+h) − θ(t+h)‖ / ‖θ(t) − θ(t+h)‖ — a predictor's forecast error divided by the error of doing nothing (hold-stale). Below 1 = the predictor beats hold-stale; = 1 = no skill; above 1 = actively harmful. h* is the largest horizon h whose median ratio is still < 1 (0 = never beats stale). In the charts below the faint blue band is the "predictor wins" region and the faint red band is "harmful"; hold-stale, being the denominator, is identically the dashed line at 1.0.

What each measurement means

Every scorecard row carries these. Weights are flattened per matrix / block / layer; a group's metric concatenates its member matrices into one vector, so a group ratio is the true joint ratio, not an average of per-matrix ratios.

weight_proj_ratio‖forecast error‖ ÷ ‖hold-stale error‖. < 1 predictor wins · = 1 no skill · > 1 harmful. The headline metric.
skill1 − ratio². The fraction of the weight displacement's energy the predictor removed — 0 at ratio 1, → 1 as the forecast becomes perfect, negative when harmful.
h* (h_star)Largest horizon h (in optimizer ticks) whose median ratio is still < 1 — how many steps ahead a forecast still beats doing nothing. 0 = never beats stale.
traj_r2R² of a straight-line fit to a matrix/group trajectory vs. tick — how linear the weight path is. Method-agnostic (computed once from the trajectory). Near 1 = nearly straight; lower = curved or noisy.
consec_delta_cosCosine between consecutive weight-update steps — directional persistence / momentum. High = updates keep pushing the same way; near 0 = each step reorients (noisy).
dir_cosCosine between the forecast's residual and the displacement it had to cover — is the leftover error aligned with the move (under/over-shoot) or sideways (wrong direction).
delta_norm · coverageHow far a group moved over the window, and the fraction of its elements that actually changed. Emitted for every group, so near-static norms and biases are never silently dropped.
stale_error · proj_errorThe two norms behind the ratio: ‖θ(t) − θ(t+h)‖ (do-nothing) and ‖θ̂(t+h) − θ(t+h)‖ (predictor). Reported as median + p10 + p90 on aggregate rows.
n_windows · in_boundsHow many valid anchor windows a cell averaged over (= 160 − h − Δ, ≥ 100 for every required cell) and whether the cell is in range — out-of-bounds cells are flagged, never silently omitted.

Science teaser #47's territory — not gated by #45

Reference readout, non-gating. Shipped only to exercise the pipeline: for naive_linear globally at Δ=20, the median ratio is 1.404 at h=40, with h* = 5 and best_delta = 5. In plain terms — naive linear extrapolation of GRPO weights beats simply holding them stale only at very short horizons (h ≤ 5) and is harmful beyond that.

A ratio > 1 or h* = 0 is a perfectly valid contract output and does not fail EXP-45. Interpreting why — and whether damped or higher-order or self-correcting predictors (#48/#49) do better — is the science of lanes #47–#49, rendered by #56. EXP-45 asserts only that the method runs and is finite.

How this compares to Linear Dynamics in the RLVR Training of LLMs — Wang et al. 2026

That paper (arXiv:2601.04537, code Miaow-Lab/RLVR-Linearity; its training codebase is adapted from verl — our own substrate) argues RLVR enters a "robust linear regime": each scalar weight and each token log-probability evolves linearly against the training step. Their linearity metric is a per-quantity OLS R² (R² > 0.7 = "strong"; median ≈ 0.8, > 70% of weights > 0.7). Critically it is RL-specific and noise-driven — plain SFT on the same base sits near R² ≈ 0.4 — and they exploit it via weight/logit extrapolation + periodic re-grounding to cut RL steps 1.6×–6.1×. That is squarely on-thesis for our communication-efficient trainer.

Are we getting similar results? Partly — and the differences are illuminating rather than contradictory. We computed our own trajectory linearity over the EXP-57 GRPO run and read it against the paper axis by axis:

How to read the partial agreement. Three differences explain most of the apparent gap, and none is a flat contradiction:

  • Different objective. Their extrapolation "win" is downstream accuracy (it can even beat the real checkpoint by dodging late-stage collapse); our weight_proj_ratio is a pure weight-reconstruction error. So our 1.404 at long h and their "accuracy improves" are not measuring the same thing.
  • Different statistic & baseline. Their R² is per-scalar over a long global slope; our traj_r2 is per-matrix and our h* comes off a local Δ=20 difference (noisier → shorter horizon expected). An R² ≈ 0.8 still leaves ~20% unexplained curvature — exactly what defeats out-of-sample extrapolation, so high R² and a short h* coexist.
  • No matching data point. The paper's only Qwen2.5-1.5B + GSM8K row is SFT (R² ≈ 0.43); it has no GRPO run at our exact config — its RL evidence is reasoning-distilled models on hard olympiad math. Its own noise-driven mechanism predicts weaker linearity on easy GSM8K (higher pass rate → less reward noise) — and our median 0.685 landing between their SFT ≈ 0.4 and RL ≈ 0.8 is consistent with exactly that.

Net: our data corroborates the paper's core picture — RLVR weight trajectories are substantially (not perfectly) linear, and extrapolation pays off only over short/moderate horizons — while serving as a corrective to a naive "weights are linear → extrapolate freely" reading. The one place we diverge (no LayerNorm-low; near-uniform per-block linearity) is most likely the per-matrix vs per-scalar difference and is a cheap follow-up for #47/#56 to settle. It also confirms a concrete design lever for the comm-efficient trainer: project/extrapolate across pipeline boundaries over short horizons and re-ground periodically — the same recipe the paper validates.

How it was computed

Delta-Gram streaming engine. The ~987 GB fp32 all-160 trace never fits in RAM, so the harness makes a single streaming pass over the ticks with a bounded rolling buffer, accumulating the Gram matrix of weight-deltas needed to compute every (method × Δ × h) cell. One pass: 22 chunks, RAM cap 40 GB, peak ~31 GB RSS, no OOM, 99.8 minutes, band 60.

Reused metric math. Every ratio / skill / dir_cos is computed through weight_proj.metrics (pinned METRIC_CONTRACT = "weight-proj-metrics-v1"), never re-derived. Off-path parity vs. the direct predictors.Order1 + metrics.full_metric_row path is 0.00e+00, so the fast surrogate is provably the same arithmetic.

Grid. methods = {hold_stale, naive_linear} · Δ ∈ {5, 10, 20} · h ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40} · operating point (20, 20) + fast example (10, 10). Window validity t−Δ ≥ 0 and t+h ≤ 159 gives n_windows = 160 − h − Δ ≥ 100 for every required cell. Determinism (soft gate) held: a re-stream produced byte-identical rows.

How the lanes plug in

#47/#48/#49 consume this harness without touching its internals. Each registers a predictor and emits one table in the same schema; #56 is the only place tables become the MOAT verdict and rendered visuals.

  • Implement a method as predict(history, delta, h) → θ̂ — history is the ordered anchors ending at θ(t) — and register_method it against moat_scorecard.py. The leakage-guarded fit_window_positions() is already wired so #49's fit method can never see the scoring point.
  • The out-of-scope families (OrderPoly / EMA / Learnable* / GeneralRegression) are intentionally not registered — the lanes bring their own.
  • No re-streaming needed. The 18 MB stats_cache.npz (fingerprint 55defd52e25fa70d) lets any linear method whose anchors fall within Δ + h ≤ 60 (band 60) be scored directly from the cached delta-Gram statistics — the 987 GB trace does not have to be read again.
