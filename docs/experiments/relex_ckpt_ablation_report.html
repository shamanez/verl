How many exact checkpoints does the two-circuit anchor need to forecast the fast network?

A CPU replay of the anchor's forward weight projector on the open-sourced RELEX RLVR checkpoint trajectory, scored by forecast skill over all 338 tensors. The short answer is a reframing: the number of checkpoints is not the deciding variable - the directional persistence of the weight updates is. That single fact explains why the live compressed run loves a 2-checkpoint secant while this dense proxy cannot be forecast by any window.

TL;DR

The anchor holds the fast network's own exact-but-delayed checkpoints and must project them forward to estimate where the fast network is now. We replayed that projector on a clean dense RLVR trajectory of the same base model and scored how well it forecasts the next checkpoint versus simply reusing the newest stale one.

The answer, precisely. "How many checkpoints" is the wrong first question. On this dense trajectory, successive cadence-spaced weight updates are almost orthogonal (a random walk), so no window beats the stale baseline and the aggressive W=2 secant is the worst (skill -1.019 vs -0.153 at W=6); larger windows help only by damping toward "do not move". The live run's W=2 win is real but contingent: the compressed PowerSGD fast circuit produces low-rank, directionally-persistent updates (direction cosine 0.91), and it is that persistence - not the checkpoint count - that makes the 2-point secant the right projector. Recommendation: keep the W=2 secant at horizon 1 as the projector for the compressed circuit (validated live), understand it as a recent-tangent extrapolator that pays off only when updates persist, and do not expect any projector to help an uncompressed circuit.

The setup in one screen

The comm-efficient GRPO trainer runs two circuits. A compressed fast learner (PowerSGD, rank 77) does the real optimizer steps. An isolated anchor never optimizer-steps and never reads the immediate fast weights; it is fed the fast network's exact but delayed checkpoints and projects them forward. From that projected state it hands back Q (the activation-energy basis PowerSGD compresses against) and M (an EMA of the clean anchor gradient, used as a sign reference in G_corr = 0.25·G_fast + 0.75·|G_fast|·sign(M)).

Cadence, delay, window, horizon

Config defaults (verl/workers/config/comm_eff.py): cadence 20, delay 20 optimizer ticks, window 4 snapshots. There are 2 optimizer ticks per global step, so the anchor fires every 10 global steps and its newest exact checkpoint is 10 steps stale. When the fast net is at step 50 the anchor holds {10, 20, 30, 40} (W=4, gap 10), projects to step 50 (horizon 1, "current fast") and could project to 60 (horizon 2, "twice a fast").

What each metric means

Every metric is the whole-tensor port of the live 16-coordinate causal probe (verl/workers/comm_eff/rank1_probe.py), computed in float64 over the tensor's elements (full tensor for the ~250 smaller tensors; a fixed 250,000-coordinate sample for the larger matrices - a 15,000x expansion of the live 16-coordinate probe). The comparison is always the same: projected weights versus "do nothing, reuse the newest stale checkpoint".

Forecast skill = 1 − proj_SSE / stale_SSE
The headline. proj_SSE is the sum of squared errors between the projected weights and the true future checkpoint; stale_SSE is the same for the stale checkpoint. Skill > 0 means the projection beats doing nothing; 1 is perfect; skill < 0 means the projection is worse than reusing the stale checkpoint (skill = −1 means it doubles the squared error). The bar the anchor must clear is simply skill > 0.
Direction cosine
Cosine between the predicted update (projected − latest) and the true update (actual − latest). Skill asks whether we moved the right distance; cosine asks whether we moved in the right direction. This is the pivotal quantity in this study.
Delta persistence (global cosine of successive updates)
The energy-weighted cosine between one gap-spaced weight update and the next (D₁ = θt-G − θt-2G vs D₂ = θt − θt-G). This is exactly what a 2-point secant assumes stays near 1: that the last move predicts the next move. Near 0 means a random walk.
In-window EVR (explained variance ratio) and R^2
How well a single rank-1 direction and a straight line describe the checkpoints already in the window. Both near 1 means a pristine in-window fit. As H3 shows, that says nothing about the forecast.
Fraction of tensors that beat stale (frac win)
Share of tensors whose individual skill is > 0. A breadth measure.
Pooled vs macro aggregation
Pooled (energy-weighted): sum SSEs across tensors, then form the ratio, so high-energy tensors dominate - the robust headline. Macro: average per-tensor skills equally, so a tiny near-frozen bias counts as much as a big matrix; it is informative but unstable for near-zero-motion tensors (their skill explodes), so we lead with pooled.

H1 - How many checkpoints?

Forecast skill versus window W at horizon 1, on the dense proxy at the cadence gap. The hypothesis was that skill peaks at small W. The data says something sharper: at this gap the projector cannot win at any W, and within that losing range the ordering is reversed from the live run - larger windows are less harmful.

H2 - Why W=2 (and why it flips)

The 2-checkpoint secant draws the line through the two newest exact checkpoints and steps one gap further along it. It bets everything on one assumption: the last weight update predicts the next one (delta persistence near 1). A W≥3 OLS instead fits a line through several deltas, which averages in older directions and damps the step.

theta_hat = latest + alpha · (h / g) · (latest − base)   (the W=2 secant)

When updates persist (are collinear), the secant's aggressive full-step extrapolation is right and W=2 wins - which is the live compressed regime (direction cosine 0.91, skill 0.78). When updates are orthogonal, the secant confidently steps in an uncorrelated direction and roughly doubles the error, while the damped W≥3 estimate that barely moves is safer. That is this dense proxy: the measured update-direction cosine is only about +0.05 to +0.20, and the energy-weighted delta persistence is essentially 0. So the very same projector that wins live is worst here. The controlling variable is persistence, not the number of checkpoints. The gap sweep below tests whether finer spacing recovers that persistence (it does not).

H3 - Is rank-1 right, and does in-window fit predict forecast skill?

For a single delta (W=2) rank-1 is trivially exact (EVR = 1). The real question at W≥3 is whether a beautiful in-window fit predicts a good forecast. It does not - and cranking the rank up, which fits the window even better, makes the forecast worse.

Decoupling, quantified. At W=4, raising the rank from 1 to 3 lifts in-window EVR from 0.854 to 0.985 yet drops forecast skill from -0.230 to -0.509. Rank-1 is not just "enough" - it is the safest choice, confirming the harness's hard rank-1 design. In-window EVR/R^2 are diagnostics of the fit, not predictors of the forecast.

H4 - How far ahead can the anchor fire (horizon)?

Horizon h is how many gaps ahead we project (h=2 = "twice a fast", which would let the anchor fire half as often). On a persistent trajectory a modest h can still beat stale; on this random-walk proxy it cannot, and further extrapolation is strictly worse.

No cadence headroom on the proxy. For W=2, skill goes -1.019 → -1.992 → -2.820 across h=1,2,3; for W=4, -0.230 → -0.424 → -0.740. Because increments are uncorrelated, doubling the horizon roughly doubles the overshoot. Any decision to fire the anchor less often must be justified in the compressed regime (where updates persist), not on this dense proxy.

H5 - Pin-to-latest vs rebuild-from-base

The harness projector is pinned to the newest exact checkpoint: it keeps latest and adds only the incremental rank-1 motion, preserving that checkpoint's off-subspace residual. The RELEX paper instead rebuilds the whole delta from the window base (theta_hat = base + c_pred · V^T), discarding that residual. They are identical at W=2 and diverge for W≥3.

Pinning wins. At W=4, pin-to-latest (-0.230) is clearly less harmful than rebuild-from-base (-0.587), because the newest exact checkpoint carries real off-subspace content that the base reconstruction throws away. Keep the pinned increment. (The H1 curves show the gap widening with W: rebuild-from-base gets steadily worse as the window lengthens.)

The gap sweep - reconciling the proxy with the live run

Persistence does not recover. Even at the finest gap (G=1, consecutive checkpoints = 2 optimizer steps), the W=2 secant skill is about -1.022 and the delta persistence is -0.010 (per-tensor direction cosine +0.172). The dense RLVR trajectory of this base model is directionally uncorrelated step-to-step at every scale we tested. The live compressed run's direction cosine of 0.91 therefore cannot come from the base model's dynamics - the most parsimonious source is the PowerSGD compression itself, which forces each update into a shared rank-77 subspace and so makes consecutive updates collinear. That is the reconciliation: the projector is a good idea because the fast circuit is compressed, not in spite of it.

Follow-up - does adding consecutive checkpoints help our projector?

The gap sweep and the RELEX paper both point at one untested corner: our study only ever used a few checkpoints (W=2 to 8). RELEX instead fits ~50-125 checkpoints sampled at every step. This follow-up isolates the checkpoint count using our own projector unchanged: feed it W = 2, 4, 8, 10 consecutive checkpoints (stride 1, from the early trajectory, steps 1-12), deltas from the window base, pinned to latest, projecting one step ahead. It does NOT reproduce RELEX (no from-the-pretrained-base reconstruction, no long horizon); it just asks whether more consecutive history sharpens our rank-1 estimate.

Interpretation and decision

  • Keep the W=2 secant at horizon 1 for the compressed circuit. The live evidence (skill 0.78, direction cosine 0.91, MATH 67.89% vs 63.61%) stands. The secant is the right recent-tangent extrapolator precisely when updates persist, which the compressed fast circuit delivers.
  • Do not read "more checkpoints" as "better". On any trajectory whose updates are not persistent, extra checkpoints only help by damping the projector toward the stale checkpoint; the honest move there is to not project at all. The number of checkpoints is a second-order knob behind persistence.
  • Fit is not forecast. In-window EVR/R^2 near 1 (and higher SVD rank) do not imply skill; here higher rank actively hurts. Trust a held-out forecast score, never the in-window fit.
  • Rank-1 is correct. The rank sweep confirms the harness's hard rank-1 choice.
  • Keep pinning to latest. Pin-to-latest beats rebuild-from-base at every W≥3 by preserving the newest checkpoint's off-subspace residual.
  • The projector's benefit is compression-specific. The persistence that makes it work is, on the evidence here, induced by PowerSGD, not by the base model. A follow-up that measures direction persistence directly on the live compressed checkpoints (vs an uncompressed control of the same run) would close this loop.

Caveats

  • Dense proxy vs compressed fast. This is the central caveat, now a finding: the RELEX trajectory is a dense (uncompressed) RLVR run and is directionally uncorrelated step-to-step, whereas the live fast circuit trains under PowerSGD compression and is collinear. The study upper-bounds what a projector can do on a clean same-base-model trajectory and isolates persistence as the deciding factor; the live probe is the ground truth for the compressed regime.
  • Weight-space, not MATH. Skill and cosine are weight-space forecast quality, not downstream accuracy. The accuracy link is the existing live result. A GPU follow-up could reconstruct a predicted checkpoint and evaluate it.
  • Step-index and spacing. RELEX step index is treated as the trajectory time axis and the gap as a free parameter bracketing the harness's 10-global-step cadence. The finest gap we can probe is 1 RELEX step (2 optimizer steps); we cannot see below that.
  • Large tensors are coordinate-sampled. The ~250 smaller tensors are scored in full; matrices above 250,000 elements use a fixed 250k-coordinate sample. Validation on held-out tensors put the sampled skill within about 0.01-0.06 of the full-tensor value and the direction cosine within 0.003, far tighter than the effects discussed.
  • Sparse anchors and a short trajectory. The genuinely-distinct RELEX region is short (steps 10-70 after excluding the repeated tail and the step-80 outlier), so large windows have few anchors and wide error bars. We report mean ± std and do not over-interpret single-anchor points.

Method and reproducibility

The projector under test is a CPU port of verl/workers/comm_eff/lookahead.py (project_rank1_tensor and Rank1RelexProjector), proven numerically identical to the live function for W=2,3,4,6 (max difference 0.00e+00). Mechanics: cumulative deltas versus the window base, rank-1 via the Gram trick, an OLS temporal fit (with the base coordinate added at W=2, giving the exact secant), and the prediction pinned to the newest exact checkpoint plus the incremental rank-1 motion.
